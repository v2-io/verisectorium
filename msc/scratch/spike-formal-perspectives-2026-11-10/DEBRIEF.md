# Debrief: what four formal languages, and five older fields, see in the model

*To Joseph, from the spike agent (Fable 5.2), 2026-11-11. You asked for exemplars in Lean 4, Datalog and one of Alloy or TLA+, and said you were "even more interested in each language's perspective on the model we have"; then widened it to anything else worth finding. This is written from the four perspective files and my own, each read whole after it was finished, not from memory of the work.*

*What was done. I read notes 22 and 23, the kernel spec (drafts 3 and 4), both builders' accounts and nearly all of `core/`, and cut the model down to the smallest thing that can run the example in note 22 §4.3 (results T and U, the auditor's finding, the dead-end repair, the rewind, S1). Then the same small model and the same story were written four times: as rules (Datalog, by me), as types and proofs (Lean 4), as relations (Alloy 6) and as states and steps (TLA+), the last three by forks of me working in parallel. I also read into five fields that met parts of this problem long ago.*

*What standing any of it has. The small model is much smaller than the kernel spec: no warrant levels, no descent, one root, nobody's standing. Everything below was found on it, on one story and a few small cases. The three forks began with my reading and my guesses, so where two languages agree it is one expectation surviving two different tests, not two minds. Nothing has been checked by anyone but its maker; the lead is commissioning that. Three of my guesses going in were wrong, and I say which. Note 23 §8 and the revisions to note 22 after the draft-4 re-runs were written while I worked; I read §8 only after this was written, and changed nothing here for it. It says of the rule for when a found defect still stands that "the right one should come from what the rule is for, not from patching four cases"; item 1 below bears on exactly that.*

## What I would put in front of you first

**1. The order the conditions need is not the clock's.** Your sentence was: "there should be causal ordering, fwiw, definitely a clock and an addative base". The kernel spec took the clock as the order (every event has a time, times are totally ordered, a piece is at the version of its latest write). Four separate faults have now been found that all come from reading something owed from *where an event sits in a line*:

- a rewording credited with having been written against a version its writer never saw, because after two forks merge it comes later by the clock (both builders found this one);
- two forks each rewriting the same piece, each stationary alone, the merged line stationary, and the later clock silently winning (the TLA+ fork found this and the Alloy fork then reproduced it; neither builder reports it);
- a found defect shed by putting the bad version back and then editing (the OCaml builder found this, and a wording that mends it);
- a check that does not count because it was made before the line had the version it was about (the OCaml builder again).

I rewrote the small model so that no rule reads the clock: each write says what its writer had in front of it (which write it replaces, what it was written against, what it was made beside), and evidence already says what it read. The story's frontier is then identical at every row, the program is no longer, the second and third faults show as owed with no clause written for them, the fourth does not arise, and the first cannot be stated. One hole is not helped: a draft found wrong, the fork rewound, the draft re-entered reworded. That is your open item on descent, and it is a writer leaving something out, which no footing repairs.

So, on small evidence: the clock is for the record, and what the conditions should read is what each event's maker saw. I think "causal ordering" in your sentence was the load-bearing half.

**2. "Consistent" is not a fixed point that has to be chosen.** Both builders report that consistency, defined as the spec defines it, has more than one fixed point and that taking the greatest is a choice. Three of the four languages, each by its own means, said the same thing about this: say it as *fault* instead. A piece is at fault when it owes something itself or rests on a piece at fault. Then consistent is "nothing it rests on, however far down, owes anything", and two pieces resting on each other with nothing wrong are simply fine. Lean proved, for any number of pieces, that this is the spec's greatest fixed point whenever what a piece owes of its own does not depend on what is consistent. The other fixed points are a piece being at fault for no reason. Core already says it this way ("unsettledness spreads along what rests on what"); note 22's condition 5 says it the other way round, and when that sentence is transcribed literally into a logic engine it has two answers.

**3. The builds' rule "a question, promise or waiver is unconditional" is three different things.**

- A question or promise that stands only while its piece is consistent has no answer at all. That is the builders' counterexample, and no tool gives it one.
- A *waiver* that stands only while its piece is consistent does have an answer under the spec's definition. Lean accepts it. What comes out is a piece with a defect, counted consistent, on a waiver that holds because the piece is consistent. The reason to forbid it is that nothing should stand on a circle, which is a different reason from the one on record.
- A decision conditioned on a piece *upstream* of what it supports is harmless, and it is exactly what note 22 §3.4 says a decision is. The rule forbids it, and both builders reported that they could not say "the named assumption failed, so the acceptance no longer supports anything". With the condition allowed, the small model says it correctly: the assumption is found false and the result owes a good acceptance, with not a word rewritten. The line that matters is not "unconditional" but "no chain of conditions and restings leads back to where it started".

**4. With "who" set aside, none of the four languages can tell your two atoms apart.** To Lean, a check and a decision are both records that someone attested something; neither can go where a proof goes, because evidence can be wrong and more of it can mean more owed. To Datalog, both are stored facts, and an affirmation or a waiver reads exactly like evidence that holds. The differences the kernel spec draws between them (evidence is known on every fork, decisions stay on theirs) are choices of the lead's and are not forced by the logic. I do not read this as a finding against the two atoms. I read it as: what distinguishes them is who may add one and who may undo it, and that is precisely the part the kernel has set aside. A kernel in which every actor counts cannot test what tells them apart. Nothing was run for this item; it is a reading across the four.

**5. Stationary is where a close lands. It is not something the verisectorium is.** Written as "the main line is always stationary", TLA+ refutes it in two states: one finding arrives. Written as "stationary with the record as it stood at its last close", it holds in all two and a half million states of a small two-fork universe. Seen that way the verisectorium has exactly two kinds of visible step: something is *learned*, and a fork *closes*. Everything else on a fork is no step at all, which is your "practical sequentialness but logical simultaneity" exactly. Two things follow that the prose blurs. "The checkpoint" names two objects: the state rested at, frozen with its record, which stays stationary for ever; and that same line read with today's record, which may not be. And a check made on a fork is on the verisectorium's record at once, though nothing the fork wrote is the verisectorium. A third thing the same view shows, which is a reading and was not run: the model promises nothing will ever *happen*. Every checkable statement is "nothing bad lands". That stabilizing gets done is entirely praxis.

**6. Your own example, run mechanically, does not close as told.** The first two of these all four languages found; the last two are the TLA+ fork's.

- By the letter, S1 is not stationary. The eli8 depends on the description; the eli8 was rewritten; nobody looked at it beside the description. The story needs one more affirmation, or one of two readings stated (writing a dependent piece counts as having looked; or a whole read counts as having looked at the parts together).
- "c1′ is fine" is not so by your own rule that a found defect is presumed present until looked for.
- The summary says the record holds "that B passed a derivation with an error in step 3". It does not: A's discovery of the error is not an event. Make it one, and a look for that error in the corrected derivation is owed before the close.
- Asked for every way the fork could close, the model checker's shortest route keeps the *old* eli8: the claim gained a hypothesis, the description was affirmed unchanged, so nothing asks anyone to look at the eli8 again. Whether affirming the middle of a chain should shield the end of it is a question about the model that I have not seen asked.

None of these is a fault in the model. They are what "computed, never typed" is for, on the model's own example.

## What each way of seeing showed

**Rules and facts** (Datalog, and two richer relatives). The nine conditions are rules. Written as a logic program they are about fifty lines; they run; and a frontier item's cause and its remedy are the two kinds of leaf of its derivation (what is present and failed; what is absent and would do), so "each naming what failed and what it would take" needs no design. Which news is good can be read off the rules without running a state: recording evidence that holds can only remove items; setting a finding aside can go either way, which one builder found by hand. I expected these tools to *enforce* "computed, never typed", and they do not: both accept a status typed by hand beside the rules that compute it. The theory draws your line; the engines leave it to whoever is watching. This view cannot say "better", cannot say who, and cannot say a refused write: every set of facts is a state, and what is wrong with it is owed.

**Types and proofs** (Lean 4). A checkpoint can be a type that cannot be filled in without a proof that nothing is owed; asking for one at 13:00 is rejected by the compiler. That works without strain. But the proof is about the bookkeeping: asked what S1 rests on, Lean answers "the definitions", and B's checks are data the proof is *about*. A typed core would certify the record-keeping and never a claim. As for carrying the model "as types": what kind of piece fills a slot, and how many, can be typing. "Required" cannot, and nothing in the nine conditions can, because a fork has to pass through states that fail them. What makes even the first part possible is your slot states: without *suggested*, *promised* and *gap* as values, a typed result could not exist until it was complete. And "the same, for this evidence" came out as something exact: if it means "do not differ in what matters" it chains and runs both ways for free, and a read that relies on existence only, normalization, and an attested sameness are one idea (evidence is about what was *seen* of a version). If it means "close enough" it cannot be built at all. Lean gave nothing on forks.

**Relations, and a solver that hunts for counterexamples** (Alloy 6). Alloy has no recursion, so it had to find the model's computed side without any, and did: canon, sameness and fault are three reachabilities and the rest is plain. Fork, rewind and close are not operations in this view; they are which set of events is being looked at. Its real contribution was on two forks. The fork tried fourteen times to say in a sentence when two forks "do not meet", and each time the solver produced a pair that satisfied the sentence and still could not both close. (A fifteenth sentence was not decided: the solver was stopped after ten minutes with no answer, which is not a result.) Most of the early ones are canon moving: canon is a reachability, and a path with one step from each fork exists on neither. Two late ones are worth keeping. A finding can be about a *combination* of versions that no line has yet, true and on record and applying nowhere until the two forks close together; that is your "unconnected contradiction" made visible before the merge. And a piece with two independent supports, where each fork removes a different one. Its reading of all fourteen: every frontier item is a conjunction, and two forks meet exactly when some item takes one conjunct from each. If so, "meeting" can be read off the conditions and need not be discovered by search.

**States and steps** (TLA+). Items 1, 5 and most of 6 above. It was no help with the conditions themselves, and time made it slow.

**Older fields.** The builders both wrote that fork-meeting "is the read-set and write-set condition of transaction processing" and that neither had checked a text. I read one: the property is called *invariant confluence* (Bailis and others, 2015), and it comes with a theorem. Where two changes are not confluent, one of them must wait for the other, in any engine whatever. So "reworked on the new checkpoint" is not a cost of this design; the design's job is to make it rare, which holding-is-not-resting does. Assumption-based truth maintenance (1986) is your "going back loses work, not knowledge", forty years old; asked its question, the record of your example says that at each moment I tried between 08:30 and 14:00 no combination of versions ever written could have been rested at, and at 14:00 exactly one could, which is S1. Build systems have evidence-with-reads and "same" under other names, and no guard, because they re-run every test on every version; which says a guard is a regression test for a world where checks are not free. Core already has that word.

## On "as types instead of terms"

The formal part of the model is not one layer. What may be stored has a shape, and types carry it. What a state owes is a function, and rules carry it. How states follow one another is steps, and a model checker explores them. Each language was good at one of these and said so plainly about the others. If core is ever to hold a formal statement beside note 22 §4.1, I would try the nine conditions as rules first, with §4.3 as their test, and I would hold that against your own caution about hand-kept machinery: fifty rules are one more thing to keep true unless they replace prose.

## What none of this establishes

That any of it holds for the kernel spec, which is several times the size of what was run. That the causal footing survives the builders' own test cases; I wrote mine from their descriptions. That the four renderings of the small model are item-for-item the same; they report the same departures from your table and I did not compare further. Nothing here touches intake, "better", delivery, or who.
