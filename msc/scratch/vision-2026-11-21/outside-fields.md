## 1. The finding I would put first: the model has a near-ancestor, and its field test has been written up

**Parnas & Clements, "A Rational Design Process: How and Why to Fake It" (IEEE TSE, 1986)** [read: §§VI–VIII whole, the rest skimmed]. I did not expect how closely this matches what `core/` is doing. In their own words:

- One home per fact: "There must be one, and only one, place for every fact that will be in the document."
- The document is where design happens: "The documentation is our medium of design and no design decisions are considered to be made until their incorporation into the documents."
- Gaps are marked where the content would go: "If a piece of information is unavailable, that fact is noted in the part of the document where the information should go."
- The body states present truth, and integration replaces what was there: "If a change invalidates a design document, it and all subsequent design documents must be faked to look as if the change had been the original design."
- Typed terms in brackets, with a mechanical check, which is what `«term»`, `‹subtype›` and `bin/defs check` are: "Each of the many terms that we must define is enclosed in a pair of bracketing symbols that reveals its type. There is a separate dictionary for each such type … The special bracketing symbols make it easy to institute mechanical checks for terms that have been introduced but not defined or defined but never used."
- History is kept apart from the body: "We make a policy of recording all of the design alternatives that we considered and rejected. For each, we explain why it was considered and why it was finally rejected."
- Newcomers are the reason for all of it: documentation written late is "useful to people who know the system well but impenetrable for newcomers," while their method means "When new programmers join the project they do not have to depend completely on the old staff for their information."

The method was applied in earnest to the Darlington nuclear plant's shutdown-system software over thirteen years. The write-up is **Wassyng & Lawford, "Lessons Learned from a Successful Implementation of Formal Methods in an Industrial Project" (FME 2003)** [read: history and all of §§3–6]. The lessons read like advance notice for this project:

- **The bracket notation was dropped because readers would not use it.** "when we tried to use these tables to present requirements behaviour, readers were quick to tell us that they probably would not use them. Domain experts especially found them close to unreadable." They turned the tables sideways (suggested by "someone new to function tables"), removed "all the bracketing symbols," and used short identifier prefixes instead. Their verdict: "arriving at notation and presentation methods that industrial participants will accept, is about as important as the technology in the formal methods themselves."
- **Precision is not the same as unambiguity, and it produces confidence that isn't earned.** In one case two groups read one mathematical requirement in opposite ways, and "Each group used the precision of the mathematical description to support its interpretation of the requirement."
- **Accepted discrepancies, which `core/` calls waivers, were a bad bargain.** It is tempting to accept "seemingly innocuous discrepancies … when fixing them would strain resources," but "it can be difficult to identify all black-box visible effects of the discrepancy … The time spent on justifying these accepted discrepancies, may be better spent in trying to 'correct' them if at all possible."
- **Rationale was lost despite a dedicated project to record it.** "rationale for some decisions that were made fifteen to twenty years ago has been lost. In at least one case, that lack of rationale led us to include a requirement that was later shown to be problematic."
- **Reopening settled decisions hurt them.** "continual second-guessing of such decisions, after the decision has been finalised, is usually destructive."
- **Why it worked, and why it costs too much elsewhere.** It worked "primarily because of the quality of the personnel involved, and the fact that … OPG was prepared to put sufficient resources into making it successful". It also worked because "the formal methods documentation and the main project documentation are one and the same". And: "the methods are just too costly without reliable, comprehensive tool support."

As far as I know (**[recall]**, not checked this session), Parnas's documentation discipline never spread much beyond the A-7E project, Darlington and a few other safety-critical settings. Industry found it too heavy. If that is right, it is the most relevant abandonment in this report: not refuted, priced out.

What I take from it: the project is in a strong position to ask *what Darlington would have needed that it lacked*. Its answer is tools and a readable notation. The engine is a tools bet. The notation lesson bears directly on `«term»` brackets and on how much of the model's surface a cold reader has to parse.

## 2. The standing objection: Naur, and the people who agree with him

**Naur, "Programming as Theory Building" (1985)** [summary, from a gist transcription; the one PDF I fetched was a scan with no text layer]. The claim is that what a programming team builds is a *theory*, meaning "the knowledge a person must have in order not only to do certain things intelligently but also to explain them", and that "the full program text and additional documentation is insufficient in conveying" it. A program "dies" when the team that holds its theory disbands. In the summarizer's quote, "program revival, that is reestablishing the theory of a program merely from the documentation, is strictly impossible." Naur recommends discarding the old text and rebuilding instead. His cases are a compiler extended by a second group whose changes broke the design, and a real-time system only its long-time staff could debug. (The summarizer's quote of the compiler case looked garbled, so I'm not reproducing it. Read the essay itself before leaning on the details.)

Three independent bodies of evidence back Naur:

- **Thurston, "On Proof and Progress in Mathematics" (1994)** [read: §§3–6]. "Mathematical knowledge and understanding were embedded in the minds and in the social fabric of the community … This knowledge was supported by written documents, but the written documents were not really primary." Also: "There were published theorems that were generally known to be false, or where the proofs were generally known to be incomplete." Which published claims to trust was known to the community and not written down. His account of foliations is the cleanest case I found of **a field dying from unlearnability rather than falsity**. He proved the main theorems, wrote them up in "a conventional, formidable mathematician's style" that "depended heavily on readers who shared certain background," and "Within a couple of years, a dramatic evacuation of the field started to take place … Today, I think there are few mathematicians who understand anything approaching the state of the art of foliations as it lived at that time." Nothing in it was false. It became unlearnable, and the people left. For geometrization he changed course on purpose: "I concentrated most of my attention on developing and presenting the infrastructure," and "the infrastructure was in my head, not in the mathematical community." He also describes drift under turnover: "as new batches of mathematicians learn about the subject they tend to interpret what they read and hear more literally, so that the more easily recorded and communicated formalism and machinery tend to gradually take over from other modes of thinking."
- **Collins, "The TEA Set: Tacit Knowledge and Scientific Networks" (1974)** [search; the details are recall]. No lab got the TEA laser working from published papers alone. The labs that succeeded had spent time with a lab that already had one working.
- **MacKenzie & Spinardi, "Tacit Knowledge, Weapons Design, and the Uninvention of Nuclear Weapons" (AJS 1995)** [search]. If weapons design stops and there's no new generation to pass tacit knowledge to, the weapons will in a qualified sense have been "uninvented." **FOGBANK** is the government-audited instance: GAO-09-385 [search] reports that NNSA had to relearn how to make a W76 warhead material because few records of the 1980s process had been kept and the staff had left. It cost about $69M and a year's delay. (One commentary argues GAO's real finding was mismanagement, not irrecoverable knowledge.)

**Where this bites here, and why it isn't only an objection.** Naur's teams persisted, and so did Thurston's community. In this project nothing persists except Joseph. Applied literally, Naur says the theory lives only in Joseph, plus whatever the text carries. So the design question isn't Parnas or Naur. It's how much of the theory the text can carry, and how fast a new mind can regrow the rest from it. Those are two different pressures on the writing. Parnas pushes toward dense reference prose ("documentation that must be read very attentively"). Thurston pushes toward "ways of thinking" and infrastructure. The estate's "teach by narrative and worked example" is on Thurston's side, and the def files are on Parnas's. I'd want the model to say which of the two each surface is for. As far as I can see, it doesn't yet.

## 3. Errors that propagate along references, losing their caveats on the way

This is the failure I found documented in the most separate fields, each with numbers. They describe one mechanism: **a later statement rests on an earlier one, restates it more strongly or more specifically, and drops its conditions.** The result looks exactly like a statement grounded in evidence.

- **Intelligence.** The 2004 Senate Select Committee report on prewar Iraq intelligence, Conclusion 4 [summary, from a full-text reprint]: the NIE "suffers from a 'layering' effect whereby assessments were built based on previous judgments" without carrying forward the underlying uncertainties. Conclusion 3 names the "collective presumption" and "group think." The 2005 WMD Commission [search] found the biological-weapons judgments rested "nearly entirely" on one source, Curveball, whom no American had interviewed, and that the NIE didn't tell readers so. This is "coherence is not convergence" as a matter of state record.
- **Biocuration.** Schnoes et al., "Annotation Error in Public Databases" (PLoS Comp Bio 2009) [summary + search]. Misannotation averaged 5–63% across six enzyme superfamilies, and was over 80% for 10 of 37 families in at least one database. Hand-curated Swiss-Prot was close to 0%. Error in NR grew from 1993 to 2005. About 85% of misannotations were **over-prediction**: a more specific function assigned than the evidence supported. Transfer from "the best match to a previously annotated sequence" compounds it. The archive also can't correct entries, because they belong to the submitters. Their remedies are machine-readable evidence codes, annotating "only at the level of function for which there is strong evidence," and a curated subset kept separate. (Gene Ontology's IEA code, "inferred from electronic annotation," which marks an inference made from an inference, is the established form of this. **[recall]**)
- **Scholarly citation.** Greenberg, "How citation distortions create unfounded authority" (BMJ 2009) [summary of the PMC full text]. He built the complete citation network for one claim (β-amyloid in inclusion body myositis): 242 papers, 675 citations. Supportive primary papers received 94% of citations to primary data, and the six critical ones received 6%. His vocabulary is worth taking as is: *citation bias*, *amplification* ("expansion of a belief system without data"), *citation diversion*, and **citation transmutation**, "the conversion of hypothesis into fact through the act of citation alone." Also *dead-end citation*, and *back-door* and *title invention*. The same patterns showed up in NIH grant proposals.<!--md-press:not-math:β-->
- **Reading the primary.** Simkin & Roychowdhury, "Read before you cite!" (2003) [search; abstract]. From how misprints propagate through citations, they estimate "only about 20% of citers read the original."
- **Clinical records.** Wang, Khanna & Najafi (JAMA Intern Med 2017) [search]. In 23,630 UCSF progress notes, 18% of the text was typed by hand, 46% copied and 36% imported. For residents, 11.8% was typed by hand. "Copy-forward" carries yesterday's findings, errors included, into today's record under today's signature. **[recall]** for the patient-safety literature on this, e.g. Hirschtick, "Copy-and-paste," JAMA 2006.

**Bearing on the model.** `core/` already has "rests on," evidence counted along independent lines, and the warning that dependent evidence counts once. What the fields add is *where* the failure concentrates: in **restatement that claims more than its source** (Schnoes's over-prediction, Greenberg's transmutation, the layering of caveats away). That suggests a specific check, applied to restates- and predicated-on references: does the restating piece claim more, or claim it with fewer conditions, than what it rests on? Greenberg's method also stands as an instrument: build one claim's reference graph and look at its shape. Amplification through a few reviews, and critical sources that get no citations, are both visible in the graph without reading a word. Swiss-Prot's result points the same way: what made the difference was a small curated subset kept apart from the bulk, not better labels on the bulk.

## 4. Doubt with no location: what happens to a challenge that can't be pinned down

**Voevodsky, "The Origins and Motivations of Univalent Foundations" (IAS, 2014)** [read: the relevant section]. Two episodes:

1. His 1992–93 paper was used by "multiple groups of mathematicians … at seminars and used it in their work and none of them noticed the mistake." He found it only in 1999–2000, when Deligne "was taking notes and checking every step of my arguments." The lemma "as stated, could not be salvaged". He proved "a weaker and more complicated lemma, which turned out to be sufficient for all applications". Narrowed to what was needed, this is Lakatos's lemma incorporation in practice. His diagnosis: "A technical argument by a trusted author, which is hard to check and looks similar to arguments known to be correct, is hardly ever checked in detail." That is very close to `core/src/impl-stopped-bound.md` (false for months, affirmed by several minds).
2. In 1998 Simpson posted a counterexample to the main result of Voevodsky and Kapranov's 1989 paper, but "he was not able to show where the mistake was in our paper." Voevodsky stayed sure of the result until 2013. "Simpson's paper created doubts in our result, which led to it being unused by other researchers, but no one came forward and challenged us on it."

The second episode is worth holding up against the model. A finding against the *whole* that names no piece ended neither in repair nor in refutation. The field quietly stopped using the result, for fifteen years, with no record of why. In `core/` terms: what does the frontier show for a contradiction that can't be attached to any piece, and who owes it? I couldn't tell from the outline whether a finding has to name a piece, or how one is raised against an assembly. Simpson's case is a good test.

Voevodsky's response was formalization. **The Equational Theories Project report (Tao et al., arXiv 2512.07087, Dec 2025)** [read: §§4.1–4.2, 4.7–4.8, 11] is the most recent large-scale account of many contributors with no mutual trust, kept honest by a mechanical checker. Two lessons carry over. First, the checker moves the trust problem to the statements: "Lean takes definitions and theorems for granted … These tools check something highly specific, a proof, against a specification. Contributors are responsible for the correctness of everything else." Compare instance-trial-2's note that "the claim in the segment's title has never been the subject of any check." Second, maintainers are the role that carries the load: "akin to that played by the principal investigator," responsible for reviewing all code and definitions and for keeping "regular logs of activities" because reconstructing "real-time impressions long after the fact just from the GitHub commit history and Zulip chat archive" is "extremely tedious". And: "it really helps to have a maintainer structure in place before the project begins." On LLMs, as of their work: "in most of the difficult implications that resisted automated approaches, we found that LLMs did not provide useful suggestions beyond what the human participants could already propose."

**The classification of finite simple groups** [search] is the long version of the same story. Tens of thousands of pages, about 100 authors, 1955–2004. Gorenstein declared it complete in 1983, having been misinformed about the quasithin case. Aschbacher and Smith closed that gap only in 2004, with roughly 1,200–1,300 pages. **[recall]** The "second-generation proof" program (Gorenstein, Lyons, Solomon) exists because the people who held the first proof are dying, and it is meant to be rewritten so a new generation can hold it. So this project's exact problem occurs in pure mathematics, at its largest scale, and the response there was a deliberate rewrite for transmission. The lesson for the accountable person: Gorenstein's declaration of a steady state rested on reports from others about parts he hadn't checked.

## 5. Handoffs: the moment a new mind takes over

**Patterson, Roth, Woods, Chow & Gomes, "Handoff strategies in settings with high consequences for failure" (Int J Qual Health Care 2004)** [read: introduction and Tables 2–3]. They observed shift handoffs in space shuttle mission control, a nuclear plant, railroad dispatch and ambulance dispatch. The findings I'd want in front of the model:

- **"All observed handoff updates were interactive, verbal, face-to-face interactions between the outgoing and incoming person."** Written-only handoff essentially did not occur in these settings. Their strategy list includes "Topics initiated by incoming as well as outgoing," "Include outgoing team's stance toward changes to plans and contingency plans," "Incoming receives paperwork that includes handwritten annotations," "Outgoing oversees incoming's work following update" (routine at NASA, rare elsewhere), and "Delay the transfer of responsibility when concerned about status/stability of process."
- **The new mind is also a detector.** "the person who is accepting responsibility has a fresh perspective, which has been shown to increase the detection of fixation errors." In NASA handoffs, "eight out of 75 questions were asked to detect errors (e.g. 'Do you know that for sure?')."
- Their list of handoff failure modes, taken from NASA research, could serve as a frontier checklist for an arriving actor: an incorrect or incomplete model of the system's state; being unaware of significant data or events; being unprepared for the effects of earlier events; failing to anticipate; lacking necessary knowledge; "dropping or reworking activities that are in progress or that the team has agreed to do"; and "creating an unwarranted shift in goals, decisions, priorities, or plans."
- Read-back and fixed ordering, the two strategies everyone recommends, were the two *not* observed.

**I-PASS (Starmer et al., NEJM 2014)** [search]. A standardized oral-and-written handoff bundle across nine hospitals was associated with fewer medical errors (24.5 to 18.8 per 100 admissions, about 23%) and fewer preventable adverse events. Secondary sources disagree about which outcome the 23% and 30% figures refer to, so check the paper before citing. What matters here is that the bundle is *standardized structure plus training plus an interactive spoken exchange*, not a better document.

**Bearing.** In this project the outgoing mind is usually gone, so the strongest strategy (questioning the person who knows) is mostly unavailable. "Stay on the line" recovers some of it, for a while. A sharper version: **the corpus has to play the outgoing operator.** It has to answer an arriving mind's "do you know that for sure?" That is roughly what a computed frontier and warrant are. Patterson's item 6, the outgoing team's *stance*, is something the model has no slot for that I could see. The mood and worries of the last mind are what the estate's compaction notes call "the mood". That is context, not truth, and the handoff literature treats it as worth carrying.

## 6. Who pays for the record, and what quality control costs

- **Grudin, "Why CSCW applications fail" (1988)** [search]. The first cause he lists is the disparity between who does the extra work and who gets the benefit. It recurs across cases, and it won the first CSCW Lasting Impact Award in 2014. This is the outline's open "recording cost" gap, and it is the oldest known killer of systems like this.
- **NASA's Lessons Learned Information System, OIG audit IG-12-012 (2012)** [search]. Project managers "do not routinely use LLIS to search for lessons," and only 12 of 28 (43%) contributed lessons. Policy on contributing had "weakened over time." **Darling, Parry & Moore, "Learning in the Thick of It" (HBR 2005)** [search; mechanics from recall] is the counterpoint. The Army's after-action reviews at the National Training Center worked because they are done immediately by the unit itself, and each lesson is tied to the *next* action, not filed in a repository. Their opening example is a company team "discovering" the same mistakes again. **[recall]**: design-rationale systems (gIBIS and its successors) were largely abandoned for the same capture cost. The August sweep covered IBIS's notation but, judging from its headings, not why it was abandoned.
- **Halfaker, Geiger, Morgan & Riedl, "The Rise and Decline of an Open Collaboration System" (ABS 2013)** [read: abstract and hypotheses]. Wikipedia's defenses against bad edits drove away good newcomers: "several changes the Wikipedia community made to manage quality and consistency in the face of a massive growth in participation have ironically crippled the very growth they were designed to manage." Their culprits are the restrictiveness of reverts and the "algorithmic tools used to reject contributions." The norms meanwhile "calcified against changes—especially changes proposed by newer editors."
- **Wikipedia, "verifiability, not truth"** [search]. A body maintained by anonymous, transient contributors set its inclusion threshold at what the next stranger can check against a cited source, not at truth. The slogan was contested and its place in the policy lead reworked in a 2012 RfC. Its status is still argued. For a project founded on truth over self, this is the clearest outside case of a deliberate retreat from truth to checkability under turnover. It's worth knowing why they made that choice before deciding not to.
- **curl and AI-generated bug reports (2025–26)** [search]. Stenberg banned reporters of "AI slop" in May 2025 and wrote "Death by a thousand slops" in July 2025. By several accounts the ban didn't stem the flow, and the bug bounty ended in January 2026. One maintainer, many fluent transient contributors, and plausible claims that are cheap to write and expensive to check: in the end he closed the channel.

**A possible inversion here.** In Grudin's world, *recording* is the expensive act. With agents, writing is nearly free. What's scarce is reading: each arriving mind's context budget, and above all the steward's attention. So the Grudin disparity may have flipped. The question becomes whose attention pays for each record, not who pays to write it. curl is the extreme case of that economics. I'd suggest the outline's "recording cost" gap be split into a writing cost (probably small now) and a reading and checking cost (the binding one, borne mostly by arriving minds and by Joseph).

## 7. Waivers accumulate

- **Vaughan, *The Challenger Launch Decision* (1996)** [search; secondary accounts only]. "Normalization of deviance": O-ring erosion was repeatedly reviewed and accepted, each acceptance was locally rational, and each flight that survived read as confirming the safety case. The Columbia board, echoing her, said Flight Readiness Review "ratified" the pattern instead of reversing it.
- **Darlington**, quoted in §1: justifying accepted discrepancies cost more than fixing them would have, and their effects couldn't be fully traced.

**Bearing.** `core/` lets a steady state rest on waivers as its stated conditions. That's honest, and it's exactly where both of these fields say things go wrong. Neither says waivers are wrong. They say accumulation goes unseen and each new waiver gets judged against the last one, not against the original standard. Measures that would show drift: the count and age of waivers per steady state, and whether a new waiver cites an earlier one as its precedent.

## 8. Keeping the rejected view: a counterpoint to "integration is replacement"

**Mishnah Eduyot 1:5–6** [search]. The question is why minority opinions are recorded when the law follows the majority. The answer: a later court may rely on the minority view (an earlier ruling can otherwise be overturned only by a court "greater in wisdom and number"). And, in Rabbi Judah's reading, so that if someone later says "I have a tradition that…," he can be told it's the recorded view of that individual, which was rejected.

**Parnas & Clements** record every rejected alternative "so that months, weeks, or even hours later, when we wonder why we did what we did, we can find out." **Darling et al.**'s company team kept rediscovering its own mistakes.

**Bearing.** The estate's rule deletes the refuted claim from the body and keeps it in history. That's compatible with all of the above as long as the rejected view can be *found by the next mind at the moment it is about to re-propose it*. Agents arrive knowing nothing and are fluent, so re-deriving a refuted idea is a predictable failure here, and it will feel like insight to the agent. Eduyot's second reason is a specific function: recognizing a resurfacing rejected claim as already rejected. I'm not suggesting rejected views go back into bodies. The question is whether a mind about to write X sees "X was considered and rejected, because…" before it writes. If that knowledge lives only in history prose it may never be read at the moment it matters. The `avoid` lists in defs are a small instance of the right thing.

## 9. One accountable person, and what that role actually involves

- **Rickover and Naval Reactors** [search; context from recall]. 1961 testimony: "Responsibility is a unique concept; it may only reside and inhere in a single individual." The program is known for running seventy years under constant crew rotation. As I recall it, it did this through personal technical command by the head (he interviewed every officer himself), written procedures, and "utterly ruthless internal honesty" (USNI's phrase). Accountability there included the head knowing the technical detail, not delegating it.
- **Brown & Duguid (1991), drawing on Orr's Xerox technicians** [search]. *Canonical* practice (the manuals) and *non-canonical* practice (war stories, improvisation) diverge, and the real knowledge sits in the second. The written record of how work is done was not how it was done. In this project the canonical record is supposed to *be* the practice, which Darlington managed ("documentation … one and the same") and most organizations don't.
- **Hutchins, *Cognition in the Wild* (1995), and Seifert & Hutchins on navigation-team error** [recall, partly confirmed by a search snippet]. A ship's navigation team makes many individual errors and almost all are caught inside the team. Members watch each other within overlapping "horizons of observation," and a member's career moves along the path information takes, so seniors have done the juniors' jobs and can see their errors. This only works if people stay long enough to move up. Agents don't move up. Every one arrives as the most junior member. So the error-catching that Hutchins describes, coming from overlapping knowledge, has to come from elsewhere here: from the record and from the steward.

**Bearing.** In every case with one accountable person and a large body of work (Linux **[recall]**, Rickover, Gorenstein, the ETP maintainers, curl), the accountable person's *attention* is what limits everything. The common fix is a persistent middle layer of trusted maintainers. This project can't have that layer, since nothing persists but Joseph. So I'd treat the steward's checking load as something to compute and show, like the frontier: what is waiting on Joseph specifically, and how fast it grows compared with how fast he can clear it. Gorenstein's 1983 declaration is the failure to design against: the accountable person certifying a whole on the strength of reports about parts.

## 10. Bodies that go stale on their own

**Shojania et al. (Ann Intern Med 2007)** [search]. Systematic reviews had a median of 5.5 years before a signal that they needed updating. 23% had one within two years, 15% within one year, and 7% already had one at publication. Cochrane's "living systematic reviews" were the response. **Lethbridge, Singer & Forward (IEEE Software 2003)** [search]: documentation is mostly out of date and engineers use it anyway. High-level documents stay useful longer, and "the closer you get to the real code, the more accurate the document must be." **Parnas, "Software Aging" (ICSE 1994)** [search]: aging from "ignorant surgery" (changes by people who don't hold the design concept) and from failing to change at all. **Gotel & Finkelstein (1994)** [search]: in a study of more than 100 practitioners, most traceability problems came from *before* a requirement entered the specification, that is, from lost provenance of why it is there. That matches Darlington's lost rationale.

**Bearing.** The model handles staleness caused by an internal change well (binding to versions). These fields add staleness caused by the *world* changing while the text doesn't: new evidence outside, the field moving on. Shojania's survival curve is a measured rate for one kind of body. I didn't see where an external clock enters `core/`'s frontier.

## 11. Is the question cut right?

"A large body of written work has to be kept true and moved forward by many minds, each of which arrives knowing nothing, can hold only a fraction, makes fluent mistakes, and leaves soon, with one person accountable." Every part of that occurs somewhere above. Three places where I think the fields would cut it differently:

1. **Kept true is half of it. Kept learnable is the other half, and the two trade off.** Thurston's foliations died true. Halfaker's Wikipedia lost its newcomers to its own quality control. Darlington's precise notation went unread until it was simplified. Parnas's documents "must be read very attentively." Every field that protected correctness at the cost of entry paid for it in who could still come in. The two failure modes, the body going wrong and the body becoming unenterable, call for different instruments, and I'd want the model to name both. Suggested restatement: *the body must stay true as stated, and must stay re-acquirable by a mind that arrives knowing nothing, within the time and attention it has; the second is not a courtesy to the first but the condition of anyone checking it.*
2. **The scarce resource is attention, not writing.** For agents, writing is cheap. The cost structure of every older field (Grudin, LLIS, rationale capture) has turned over. What's scarce is each arriving mind's context and the steward's checking time, and the dominant risk is fluent material arriving faster than anyone can read (curl, copy-forward notes, citation amplification).
3. **What dies at turnover is mostly not facts.** It's theory (Naur), stance (Patterson's item 6), rationale (Darlington, Gotel & Finkelstein), the knowledge of which written claims are known to be bad (Thurston), and the memory of what was already tried and rejected (Eduyot, Parnas, the AAR rediscoveries). The model is built around claims and decisions. Two of those five (rationale; rejected alternatives) fit decisions with backing. Stance, and community knowledge about reliability, I'm less sure have a home. Warrant may cover the second if it really is computed from recorded checks. Thurston's point was that this knowledge mostly wasn't recorded anywhere.

What I did **not** find anywhere: a field that has kept a large written body true *inside the body itself*, with warrant computed from recorded checks, under total turnover and one accountable person. The pieces come close: GO evidence codes, GRADE, Wikipedia's citation tags, Lean blueprints with dependency graphs colored by status, Darlington's integrated documentation. Mathematics, the field with the most at stake, kept that knowledge socially (Thurston) or moved to full formalization (Voevodsky, ETP). I didn't search for this exhaustively, so it's an absence I noticed, not a result.

## 13. On the brief

It worked well for me. Pointing at the August sweeps while saying you hadn't read them, and preferring what they didn't cover, was the most useful sentence in it. It aimed me at a different axis instead of a re-survey. "Failures interest me at least as much as successes" changed which sources I went after. One thing I'd have liked: a sentence on what "moved forward" means in the problem statement. I covered "kept true" far more than "moved forward" (delivery, results others build on), and I'm not sure whether that gap is mine or the problem statement's. The outline says delivery is the model's least exercised half, so if forward motion is the half you most need outside light on, a second pass aimed there might be worth it.
