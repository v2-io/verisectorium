## 1. The short answer

The fields that study this mostly do not describe it as reading. Cold arrival at a large body of material becomes good action through a loop: a frame, an attempt, a check, a revised frame. The material helps to the extent that it supplies a usable frame early, lets the reader find what the current attempt needs, and tells the reader how far to trust what they find. Three findings recur across software engineering, text comprehension, human factors and the recent agent work:

1. **The same content is signal or noise depending on when it arrives in that loop, and to whom.** Your note 28 already says this ("precision a mind has nothing yet to hang on arrives as noise"). The literature supports it strongly, and adds that the hooks are often made by *acting*, not only by reading a scaffold.
2. **Noise is not one thing, and the most harmful kind is near-truth.** Material that looks relevant but is stale, superseded or unadjudicated costs far more than plainly irrelevant material. For agents there is now a fourth kind: unnecessary instructions, which agents dutifully carry out.
3. **The reader in the middle of acting is a different problem, and it is well studied.** Under the names prospective memory, transfer, procedural compliance and, recently, instruction survival under context compaction. Its bottleneck is not knowing the principle but noticing, at the moment, that it applies. What helps is storing a principle with the situation it applies to, in that situation's vocabulary, and re-presenting it from outside the actor's memory. Task-success metrics do not see this failure.

On measurement: nobody has a good single measure of time-to-acting-well. There are serviceable partial ones, and the agent setting makes one design cheap that is expensive with humans: comparing performance given a focused relevant excerpt against performance given the whole corpus, at a fixed budget. That gap is the closest thing to an operational "truth to noise ratio" for a given reader that I found, though no source calls it that. Section 5 has it.

## 2. Where the fields state the problem differently

Each field below studies something close to your question and treats a different thing as the bottleneck. I list them because the choice of field decides which failures you can see.

- **Program comprehension and onboarding (software engineering).** The bottleneck is building a mental model of an existing artifact. Classic cognitive models: Brooks's top-down hypotheses confirmed by "beacons"; Soloway & Ehrlich's programming plans; von Mayrhauser & Vans's integrated model, in which readers switch between top-down and bottom-up as knowledge allows (from memory). One large field study (Xia et al. 2018; 78 professionals, 7 projects) found developers spending about 58% of their time on comprehension, with seniors spending less than juniors. The newcomer literature (Sim & Holt 1998; Begel & Simon 2008; Dagenais et al. 2010; Steinmacher's work on open-source barriers) treats arrival as social and situated, not textual.
- **Naur, "Programming as Theory Building" (1985).** This is the sharpest challenge to your premise, and I think it should be faced directly rather than cited in passing. Naur argues that a program's real substance is a *theory* held by the people who built it, and that the text and documentation cannot carry it. In his account a program "dies" when the team holding the theory dissolves, and reviving it from documents is essentially impossible. He names three areas where the holder's knowledge exceeds any documentation: how the program maps to the world, why each part is the way it is, and how to respond to a modification request. On justification, the final basis "must always remain the programmer's direct, intuitive knowledge or estimate" (fragment via the fetcher; paraphrase otherwise). A verisectorium is, in effect, a bet that a record built a certain way can carry much more of the theory than Naur's programs did. His three areas read like a list of what such a record would have to hold. Your "decisions with backing" goes straight at the second. The third, judging whether a new demand resembles an existing capability, is the one I would worry about.
- **Text comprehension (cognitive psychology).** The bottleneck is building a *situation model* beyond the text itself (Kintsch's construction-integration model, from memory). The key finding for you is the interaction between coherence and knowledge. McNamara and Kintsch, in two 1996 papers, found that low-knowledge readers learn more from explicit, highly coherent texts, while high-knowledge readers sometimes learn more from texts *with* coherence gaps, because the gaps force inference. Educational psychology's "expertise reversal effect" (Kalyuga et al. 2003) generalizes this: guidance that helps novices can hurt more experienced learners. Both support your reader-relativity. They also complicate "minimize what the reader must get through," because for a knowledgeable reader ease of passage is not the same as depth of model.
- **Information foraging (HCI; Pirolli & Card 1999, from memory).** The bottleneck is how fast valuable information is gained per unit of time, given a cost of moving between "patches" and a cost of exploiting each one, with navigation guided by "information scent" (cues that predict value before you pay for it). This is the field that already has a *ratio* in roughly your sense, and Section 4 leans on it. It has been applied to programmers: Lawrance et al. built an executable scent model that predicted debugging navigation better than models without scent. As far as my searches went, nobody has applied it carefully to LLM agents.
- **Sensemaking.** Russell, Stefik, Pirolli & Card (CHI 1993) treat sensemaking as a "learning loop": search for a representation, instantiate it with data, shift it when it fails. Representations are chosen to cut the cost of the task at hand. Klein's data/frame theory makes the frame primary: data evoke frames, frames select data, and expertise is largely skill at questioning and replacing frames. Weick's organizational sensemaking (from memory) adds that people act in order to find out what they think.
- **Situation awareness and handoffs (human factors, medicine, aviation).** This is the field that is literally about a mind arriving cold into an ongoing situation and needing to act within minutes. Endsley's three levels (perceive, comprehend, project) and the SAGAT freeze-probe measure. Shift handover is its most rigorously evaluated intervention. The I-PASS handoff bundle (Starmer et al., NEJM 2014; nine pediatric residency programs, 10,740 admissions) cut medical errors from 24.5 to 18.8 per 100 admissions (23% relative) and preventable adverse events by 30%, with no change in non-preventable events. It is the best example I found of a cold-arrival intervention measured on *downstream outcomes* rather than comprehension.
- **Transfer of learning and inert knowledge (education).** Whitehead's "inert ideas"; Gick & Holyoak's analogical transfer; Barnett & Ceci's taxonomy of far transfer. This field owns your second reader, and it gets its own section below.
- **Context engineering for LLMs (the recent agent work).** Anthropic's own framing is "the smallest possible set of high-signal tokens that maximize the likelihood of some desired outcome," with the model treated as having an "attention budget." That is your ratio in other words. The empirical work under this name (long-context benchmarks, context-file studies, compaction studies) is young but directly on point.

## 3. What makes arrival fast or slow

### 3.1 Evidence from human newcomers

Dagenais et al. (2010; 18 experienced developers joining established projects at IBM; grounded theory) is the study closest to your question, so I read it whole. Its conclusions, with the details that matter for you:

- **Three factors:** *early experimentation* (small real tasks from day one), *internalizing structures and cultures* (the tacit part, which takes the longest), and *progress validation* (frequent checks that you are heading the right way). Newcomers given code tasks on the first day "oriented themselves more quickly." Up-front courses and general documentation were "seldom ideal." When newcomers were sent to read docs, it was often "for the wrong reasons," such as the team not being ready for them or access not yet granted.
- **Timing decided usefulness.** One participant put it this way: an architecture explanation on day one "didn't really hit home until later," because the word "services" did not yet connect to anything he had seen. Given "a week or two later … it would have connected in my mind with a lot of things I'd already seen." This is your "nothing yet to hang on," observed in the field. Note where the hooks came from: work on the code, not a better scaffold.
- **The most effective pattern was a brief overview and then a low-level task**, not pure top-down or pure bottom-up. When a team forgot the overview, the newcomer "tried hard to obtain this overview through questions and reading."
- **Newcomers could not tell which documents were outdated**, "because they knew little about the past and current states of the project." One got round this by checking source history. This is the credence problem from your note 30 ("unable to tell the checked from the merely stated"), seen from the newcomer's side.
- **Trails beat maps.** Detailed bug and commit histories linking a change to its reason were among the best aids to tacit knowledge. They failed when entries were trivial ("Bug Fixed") or not linked to the change.
- **Pointers delivered just in time were valued over pointers "presented en masse in a document or meeting at the beginning."** Newcomers without progress validation "missed important information they did not know they had to know."
- **What worked across all three factors:** previous experience, examples, a mentor, and questions to colleagues. A questionnaire embedded in an architecture document helped one newcomer find what he hadn't understood.
- **Their metaphor, which I find useful:** signposts matter most "at cross-roads, i.e., places with choices where others have tended to get lost." That suggests locating orientation aids empirically, by where past arrivals actually went wrong, not by where the author thinks the structure is.

Carroll's minimalist instruction work (the 1987 Minimal Manual studies; *The Nurnberg Funnel*, 1990) found that an action-first manual much shorter than a commercial one produced more efficient learning. The original abstract says "more efficient" and gives no percentage; I couldn't verify the "40% faster" figure that circulates. Mark Baker's commentary points out that the experiments also showed that purely action-first instruction does not work alone. The same result again: action plus a frame.

### 3.2 Evidence from AI agents

- **Repository context files (Gloaguen et al., ICLR 2026).** This is the most directly relevant measured result for an AI cold reader. Across Claude Code with Sonnet-4.5, Codex with GPT-5.2 and GPT-5.1-mini, and Qwen Code, LLM-generated context files did not improve success on SWE-bench Lite or the authors' new benchmark of 138 tasks from 12 repositories that have developer-written files, and they raised cost by roughly 20 to 23%. Developer-written files gave about +2.4 points on average, not significant. For Sonnet-4.5 specifically, developer files *lowered* success on their benchmark, from 73.2 to 70.3. The details matter more than the headline:
  - Repository overviews did not shorten the number of steps before the agent first touched a file the reference fix changed. One model kept re-reading context files that were already in its context.
  - Agents followed instructions closely: mentioning a tool raised its use from under 0.01 to 1.6 calls per task. The authors' conclusion is that "unnecessary requirements from context files make tasks harder."
  - With the repo's other documentation removed, the LLM-generated files *helped*, by +2.7%. The authors read this as the files mostly duplicating existing docs. Restatement was worth something only when the original was out of reach.
  - Caveat: these are Python bug-fix tasks where the code is readable ground truth and models already know the tooling. A verisectorium is a setting where the record *is* the ground truth, so the overview's value could be very different there. I'd treat this as evidence about redundancy and unnecessary instructions, not as evidence that overviews are useless.
- **Agents with file tools beat reading (Cao et al., March 2026).** Off-the-shelf coding agents given a corpus as a directory tree, plus a question, beat the published state of the art on several long-context benchmarks: 88.5 versus 80.0 on BrowseComp-Plus. They developed iterative query refinement "without explicit instruction," wrote counting scripts instead of reading broadly, and read targeted line ranges. Folder structure beat one big file. For an agent with a fixed budget, arrival looks like foraging with tools, and the structure and scent of the corpus matter more than its read-through length.
- **Long-context degradation.** Chroma's "Context Rot" (18 models) found reliability falling with input length even on simple tasks. Distractors (topically related but non-answering material) hurt more than irrelevant material, and the harm grew with length. A focused prompt of about 300 tokens beat the full one of about 113k on LongMemEval for every model. One oddity I would not over-read: needles were found *better* in shuffled haystacks than in coherent ones, and the authors give no tested explanation. NoLiMa (ICML 2025) found that when the question and the relevant passage share little vocabulary, 11 of 13 models fell below half their short-context score by 32k tokens. Shi et al. (2023) showed that a single irrelevant sentence substantially degrades math reasoning.

### 3.3 What I take from 3.1 and 3.2 together

My synthesis, marked as such. For humans, arrival speeds up when early action manufactures hooks and frequent checks keep the reader from going far astray. For agents, the analogue of early experimentation is question-driven foraging with tools. In both, the body of work helps through (a) a short frame that is *revisable*, (b) scent and structure that make the right patch cheap to find, (c) trails from decisions to reasons, and (d) visible trust state. It hurts through near-duplicate, stale or unadjudicated material and through requirements the reader will obey without needing them. What I can't find evidence for, either way, is whether an agent oriented by *reading a scaffold, acting on a small task, then re-reading* beats the same budget spent reading. The human evidence predicts it would. It is cheap to test.

## 4. Your ratio, examined

### 4.1 There is a real quantity close to it, and it adds three things

Foraging theory's central quantity (Pirolli & Card, from memory) is a rate of gain: value obtained divided by time spent moving between sources plus time spent within them. It is a real ratio of roughly your shape, and it brings in three things your current wording leaves out:

1. **The denominator includes navigation, not only reading.** A short corpus can still be expensive if the reader cannot tell where to go. Duggan & Payne found that the benefit of skimming depended on how easily readers could navigate between pages.
2. **Value is relative to the task as well as the reader.** The same reader foraging for "can I trust claim X" and for "what should I build next" faces different ratios over the same corpus. So the ratio belongs to reader × task × budget, not to the corpus, and not to the reader alone. Note 28's "specific reader (an individual, not an abstraction)" is half of this. The task is the other half.
3. **Scent.** Foraging assumes readers can estimate value cheaply before paying for it. For a verisectorium the scent that seems to matter most is credence: whether a piece is settled, contested, superseded or raw. Without it, near-truth cannot be told apart from truth until it has been read, so it all costs the same.

### 4.2 The numerator can be negative, which bears on "truer before shorter"

Decision theory's value of information (Howard 1966, from memory) defines the value of reading something as the expected improvement in the reader's subsequent decision. Read that way, false content, and true content held with miscalibrated confidence, has *negative* value: it makes decisions worse. A ratio with a signed numerator produces "truer before shorter" naturally in most cases, because a falsehood costs far more than the extra length of the truer version.

It does not produce it in every case, and this bears on the open question in the addendum (§4 point 3): is the ratio a real quantity, or two aims in a fixed order? Under value of information, a longer and fully true text can be worse for a budget-limited reader than a shorter text with a small marked error, if the reader runs out of budget before reaching the decisive truth in the longer one. If "truer before shorter" is meant to hold even then, it is a lexical ordering and not a ratio, and the addendum is right. If it is meant as "falsehood is very expensive," a signed ratio captures it. I don't know which Joseph means. The literature doesn't decide it, but it does make the two readings distinct enough to ask about.

### 4.3 What the evidence says "noise" is made of

Separating these seems useful because their costs differ and so do their remedies:

| Kind | Evidence about its cost | What reduces it |
|---|---|---|
| Irrelevant material | Hurts LLMs measurably (Shi; Chroma), but less than distractors; cheap for humans if scent is good | Structure and scent; layering |
| Redundant restatement | Added cost without benefit when the original was reachable; helped when it was not (Gloaguen) | Restatement tied to its original (as your note 30 has it), used where the original is out of reach |
| Near-truth: stale, superseded, unadjudicated, subtly different versions | The most harmful kind in Chroma; newcomers can't detect staleness (Dagenais); low-vocabulary-overlap retrieval collapses (NoLiMa) | Replacement rather than accumulation; visible trust state |
| Unneeded requirements and instructions | Agents obey them, which raises cost and sometimes lowers success (Gloaguen; IFScale) | Fewer standing instructions; a reason attached to each |
| Precision with nothing to hang on | Reader-relative (McNamara & Kintsch; expertise reversal; Dagenais's timing finding) | Layering; delivery when a hook exists |
| False content | Negative value, as above | Adjudication |

A point I think matters and haven't seen stated: for an agent, imperative noise is worse than descriptive noise, because the agent acts on it. A human skips an irrelevant rule. An agent runs the extra tests.

### 4.4 A doubt about the ratio itself

McNamara & Kintsch's high-knowledge readers learned more from texts with gaps. If what you want from an arriving mind is a deep situation model, and not only a fast correct action, then minimizing what they must get through is not always the goal. Some effort is the mechanism (the testing-effect and desirable-difficulty work, from memory, points the same way). This doesn't overturn the ratio for the budget-limited agent who must act. It does suggest the ratio is the right target for that reader and possibly the wrong one for a mind meant to *hold* the theory, such as a future lead.

## 5. Measurement: what exists and what I would try

What exists:

- **Time to a first meaningful act.** Time to first commit; developer relations' "time to first hello world"; and Gloaguen's "steps before first touching a file changed by the reference fix," which is the cleanest agent version I found. These are cheap and objective, and they measure finding, not understanding.
- **Freeze-probe comprehension (SAGAT).** Stop the actor and ask questions about the situation. It is widely used, and the validity debate is instructive: de Winter et al. (2018) list six problems, among them that it measures what can be explicitly recalled, and correlations with performance range from r = 0.66 down to non-significant. Endsley's answer is that validity depends on asking the queries that actually matter for the task. A cold-reader quiz has the same weakness: it can measure recall of the corpus rather than readiness to act.
- **Outcome measures downstream.** I-PASS measured errors and adverse events, not handoff quality. This is expensive and slow, and it is the only kind that settles whether arrival worked.
- **Elicited models.** Dagenais had participants sketch their project as if introducing a peer; the sketches matched the interviews. Healthcare uses teach-back. These are cheap with agents: ask the arriving agent to say what it believes the state is and how sure it is of each part.
- **Navigation models.** Foraging models predict where readers go (Lawrance et al.), which would let you test whether a restructuring changes paths, without running full tasks.

What the agent setting makes cheap, which I'd try, in order of what I think it would teach:

1. **Focused versus full at a fixed budget.** Chroma's design: the same questions, with (a) only the relevant excerpt, versus (b) the whole corpus or a budget-limited view of it. The gap, or the budget needed to close it, is an operational noise cost for that reader class on that task set. It needs ground truth about what the relevant excerpt is, which is circular for live work. It isn't circular for a held-out set of questions whose answers the steward already knows.
2. **An orientation curve.** Performance on a fixed probe-and-task set as a function of tokens read, with two reference points: a no-read baseline (what the model already knows; a contamination check) and the focused condition from item 1 as a ceiling. The area under the curve, or the budget needed to reach some fraction of the ceiling, compares corpus versions. Measuring each version separately would show whether a change raised the ratio for that reader.
3. **Credence probes, not only correctness probes.** Ask the arriving reader for each belief and how confident it is, and score calibration against the record's actual check state. This measures the "calibrated truth with calibrated credence" half of note 30 directly. I found no one doing this for documentation. It seems obvious once stated, which makes me suspect I missed it.
4. **Act-first versus read-first** (end of §3.3), as an experiment between these conditions.

Two cautions from the sources. Several papers saw large run-to-run variance (instruction drift "requires averaging across many conversations"; SAGAT validity rises with aggregation). Single runs will mislead. And task success can hold while principle-adherence collapses (next section), so the measures have to include violations, not only successes.

## 6. The reader in the middle of acting

The fields and their names for this reader: prospective memory (remembering to act on an intention when its moment comes); transfer and inert knowledge (having knowledge and not using it where it applies); procedural compliance and checklists (aviation, surgery); situation awareness failures at the perception level; and, in the agent world, instruction drift, instruction density and instruction survival under compaction.

What is known:

- **The bottleneck is noticing that a principle applies, not knowing it.** In Gick & Holyoak's classic experiments, people who had just read a story containing the solution principle used it spontaneously about 30% of the time, against roughly 10% with no story. A simple hint to use the story added about 50 percentage points (figures from a later review, Pedone et al. 2001). They had the knowledge. What failed was retrieval at the moment it applied.
- **Retrieval depends on the overlap between how something was stored and how the moment looks.** This is encoding specificity and transfer-appropriate processing (from memory). Knowledge filed by topic is retrieved by topic; knowledge filed by situation is retrieved by situation. "Conditionalized knowledge," knowledge stored together with the contexts where it applies, is the education literature's name for what is missing in inert knowledge. NoLiMa is the LLM analogue: when the question and the relevant passage share little vocabulary, retrieval collapses as context grows. A principle written in the theory's vocabulary is unlikely to fire in a moment described in the task's vocabulary.
- **Cues can carry the load.** In Einstein & McDaniel's multiprocess framework, a *focal* cue (one the ongoing task already makes you process) triggers retrieval spontaneously, without effortful monitoring. Implementation intentions, "if situation X, then I do Y," improved goal attainment over plain intentions with d = 0.65 across 94 studies (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006). They work by binding the action to a situational cue in advance.
- **Aviation's design (from memory; I confirmed only that Degani & Wiener's report exists).** A very small set of "memory items" is trained until automatic, for situations with no time to read. Everything else is read from a checklist once the situation is recognized. The design concedes that the hard part is recognition, and keeps the in-head set small so that recognition and those few items stay reliable.
- **For LLMs, standing instructions decay and crowd each other out.** Li et al. (COLM 2024) measured significant instruction drift over long dialogs and attributed it partly to attention decay away from the system prompt. IFScale found the best frontier models at 68% adherence with 500 simultaneous instructions, with a bias toward instructions that came earlier. Schmied et al. found a "knowing-doing gap": models produced correct rationales 87% of the time, and then, of those cases, 64% still took the greedy action anyway.
- **Compaction is your second reader, made literal.** "Benchmarks Are Surprisingly Compaction-Friendly" (October 2026) found task performance largely unaffected by compaction on its benchmarks. Yet the first compaction dropped about a third of the anti-cheating rules, and in real user sessions, instructions about *how to work and what not to do* were dropped about 6× more often than specifications of *what to build*. The reason given: progress lives on disk and can be recovered, "except constraints that don't exist anywhere else." "Lost in Context Compaction" (2026) found compactors keeping 17% of injected side constraints on average. In one inbox scenario an agent deleted emails without approval in 30 of 100 sessions after compaction and in none before. Storing the constraints separately and re-attaching them after compaction restored retention to over 90%. I have only the abstracts of these two papers, through the fetcher.

What follows from this, as I read it:

1. Having once read a principle is weak evidence that it will be applied. The literature is about as consistent on this as on anything here.
2. What helps is (a) storing the principle as *condition → action*, (b) writing the condition in the vocabulary of the moment as it looks from inside, (c) re-presenting it at the moment from something outside the actor's memory (a checklist, a cue, a pinned constraint, a hook), and (d) keeping the set that must live in the head small.
3. **Your first and second readers pull against each other.** Protecting the second reader by loading every principle up front costs the first reader's budget, which is the context-file and IFScale result. The known resolution is aviation's split: a few always-present items, and the rest triggered by recognized situations.
4. **This failure is invisible to outcome metrics.** The compaction paper is the cleanest evidence: tasks succeeded while rules vanished. Measuring the second reader means counting violations against situations where a known principle should have fired. That means building such situations into tests on purpose, a kind of SAGAT for principles.

One observation about your own estate, in case it is useful and not merely recapitulation. Joseph's global memory already writes "BODY-SIGNAL" lines for its key principles: descriptions of what the moment feels like from inside. And it already holds that bare pointers "inoculate" against looking. The literature above gives a mechanism for both. A body-signal is a retrieval cue written in the moment's vocabulary, which is encoding specificity put to work. A pointer is a principle with no conditions attached, which is inert knowledge by construction. The same literature warns that the always-loaded surface has a capacity cost, and that hooks (cue-triggered injection) are the outside-the-head mechanism the cue literature favors.

## 7. The fifteen-minute expert

Less studied than I expected. What bears on this reader:

- **Skimming under time pressure works for the main ideas, not for inferences or details.** Duggan & Payne (2009) gave readers time for about half of each document. Skimming improved memory for important ideas but not for less important details or for inferences. It was no better than reading the first or second half of every paragraph, and its benefit depended on how easily readers could navigate. Their eye-tracking follow-up (CHI 2011) supports a satisficing account: readers keep going while information gain stays above a threshold, then jump ahead. The design implications follow directly: put the main claim of each unit first, and make the units easy to navigate between.
- **Expertise reversal applies directly.** What helps the cold agent (explicit connection, restatement, definitions) is a cost to an expert who already has the frame. The expert reader probably needs a different layer, or a way to skip the explanatory one.
- **Experts arrive with frames and test them quickly** (Klein's data/frame theory). The risk is fitting the material to the wrong frame and keeping it. Stating the work's own frame plainly at the top lets the expert accept or reject it in a minute, rather than spending ten reconstructing it.
- **My inference, not from a source:** what an outside expert can do best in fifteen minutes is calibrate trust. Pick one claim in their field, follow it to its backing, and judge. If that's right, the expert's ratio depends less on how short the body is than on how few steps it takes to get from any claim to its evidence and check state.

## 8. Doubts, and what I'd want checked

- Most of the human evidence measures learning or recall, not action quality. The software-engineering onboarding evidence is qualitative and small (Dagenais's 18). The best outcome-measured study (I-PASS) is about handoffs between humans in a domain with very different structure.
- The agent evidence is mostly Python software engineering, where the code itself is readable ground truth. A body of theory, where the record is the ground truth, may behave differently. I'd especially not carry "overviews don't help" over without testing.
- Several key 2026 papers I saw only as abstracts through a summarizer: both compaction papers, and parts of context rot. The numbers I quote are the abstracts' numbers.
- **The convergence in §6 feels clean, and that is a reason for caution.** Prospective memory, transfer, checklists and compaction all point to conditionalized, cue-triggered, outside-the-head storage. The sources do say this. Whether it holds for LLM agents beyond the compaction and instruction-density results is untested by anything I found.
- I did not find, and would want someone to look for: (a) any study measuring *calibration* of a cold reader's beliefs about a documentation corpus; (b) foraging theory applied rigorously to LLM agent traces; (c) quantitative studies of "knowledge continuity" when a team turns over completely, Naur's dead-program case, beyond anecdote. The organizational memory literature (Walsh & Ungson 1991, from memory) may hold some of this. I didn't get to it.

## 9. Feedback on the brief

The open framing worked for me. It let me follow situation awareness and compaction, which I wouldn't have reached from "documentation." One thing that would have helped me look more specifically: some sense of what "acting well" means for the arriving agent in a verisectorium (auditing? spiking? integrating?), because the literature splits sharply between finding, understanding and refraining. I'd still have wanted it as context, not as scope. I'm available for follow-up questions.
