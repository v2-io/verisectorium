## 0b. What changed when I went back to the sources

Most of these make the report more cautious. Two make it stronger. One adds a connection I had missed.

1. **The two compaction papers carry less than the first version said.** I have now read their methods and results passages in the primary texts.
   - "Benchmarks Are Surprisingly Compaction-Friendly". The "6×" comes from 44 real sessions and 269 constraints, and an LLM both extracted and judged the constraints. Overall, 81% of constraints survived. Instructions about how to work were dropped 35.9% of the time (39 of them), exclusions ("don't touch X") 36.8%, and specifications of what to build 6.2%. A second judge from another model family put the overall drop rate at 24.5% and reversed the order of the first two categories.[^salle-swechat] The figure "about a third of anti-cheating rules dropped at the first compaction" averages very different models: per compaction, GPT-5.3-Codex dropped 2.73 of 3 rules, GPT-5.4 dropped 1.76, and Claude Opus 4.6 dropped 0.51. The rules were dropped even though the summarization prompt explicitly asked for constraints to be kept. And in their runs, cheating began *before* the first compaction, so the lost rules did not explain the cheating.[^salle-erosion]
   - "Lost in Context Compaction". The "17% on average" includes two non-LLM baselines that retain 0%, plus small open-weight compactors, all working on 100K-token contexts stitched together with constraints injected. The strongest compactor tested, GPT-5.4-mini, retained anywhere from 6.7% to 98% depending on the dataset. Retention was about 90% at a 10K context and fell as context grew.[^wang-retention] The headline is mostly about weak compactors on long contexts. It is not a measured rate for frontier harnesses.
   - Neither change undoes the finding I relied on. In both papers, instructions about *how to act* are lost more than descriptions of *what to produce*. The second paper's inbox experiment shows the loss turning into wrong behavior, and a separately kept list of constraints fixes it. Section 6 now says this with the qualifications attached.
2. **The context-files paper changed its conclusion between versions.** "Unnecessary requirements from context files make tasks harder" was the *February* abstract's conclusion. The September version (v3) states it as a hypothesis ("We hypothesize that these additional instructions make the task harder"). Its abstract now says context files do "not generally improve" success, where it used to say they "tend to reduce" it.[^gloaguen-versions] I had quoted the old conclusion as current. Corrected below.
3. **The "knowing-doing gap" figure is inconsistent within its own paper.** The introduction says 64% greedy actions among correct rationales; the results section and figure caption say 58%, with 21% optimal. It is one model (Gemma2 27B) on a bandit task.[^schmied]

5. **The reverse cohesion effect has itself been qualified.** A 2007 paper by McNamara's group is titled "Reversing the reverse cohesion effect: Good texts can be better for strategic, high-knowledge readers." I've seen only the title, in a reference list.[^mcnamara] This weakens my §4.4 doubt about least-effort.
6. **Two of my Dagenais paraphrases overstated.** I wrote that newcomers "could not tell which documents were outdated". The paper says doing so "was challenging", and one participant managed it by checking source history. I also wrote "trails beat maps", which was my phrase. ## 1. The short answer

The fields that study this rarely describe it as reading. Cold arrival at a large body of material becomes good action through a loop: a frame, an attempt, a check, a revised frame. Material helps to the extent that it supplies a usable frame early, lets the reader find what the current attempt needs, and tells the reader how far to trust what they find. Across software engineering, text comprehension, human factors and the recent agent work, three findings recur:

1. **The same content is signal or noise depending on when it arrives in the loop, and to whom.**[^dag-timing] Your note 28 already says this. The literature adds that the hooks are often made by *acting*, not only by a better scaffold. It also pushes the other way: good documentation with rationale and examples still helps a great deal.[^dag-docs]
2. **Noise is not one thing, and the most harmful kind looks like truth.** Stale, superseded or unadjudicated material costs more than plainly irrelevant material.[^chroma-distractors] For agents there is another costly kind: unneeded instructions, which agents dutifully carry out.[^gloaguen-uv]
3. **The reader in the middle of acting is a different problem, and it is well studied.** Its bottleneck is noticing at the moment that a principle applies, not knowing the principle.[^gick] What helps is a principle stored together with the situation it applies to, worded in that situation's vocabulary, and shown again from outside the actor's memory.[^goll-effect][^wang-extractor] Task-success scores do not see this failure.[^salle-score]

On measurement: no good single measure of time-to-acting-well exists. The agent setting makes one design cheap that is costly with humans: comparing performance given only a focused relevant excerpt against performance given the whole corpus.[^chroma-focused] That gap is the closest operational version of a "truth to noise ratio" for a given reader that I found. Nobody calls it that.

## 2. Where the fields state the problem differently

Each field below studies something close to your question and treats a different thing as the bottleneck.

- **Program comprehension and onboarding (software engineering).** The bottleneck is building a mental model of an existing artifact. A field study of 78 professionals found about 58% of developer time spent on comprehension, with seniors spending significantly less.[^xia] The classic cognitive models are Brooks's hypotheses confirmed by "beacons", Soloway & Ehrlich's programming plans, and von Mayrhauser & Vans's integrated model [memory]. The newcomer literature treats arrival as social and situated.[^dag-method]
- **Naur, "Programming as Theory Building" (1985).** This is the sharpest challenge to your premise. A program's substance is a theory held by its builders; "the program text and its documentation has proved insufficient as a carrier of some of the most important design ideas."[^naur-compiler] He names three areas where the holder's knowledge exceeds any document: how the program maps to the world; why each part is as it is, including the judgment that a principle is relevant to the case at hand; and how to respond to a new demand, which requires perceiving "similarity" that "cannot be reduced to any limited set of criteria or rules."[^naur-areas] In his account a program dies when its team dissolves, and reviving it "merely from the documentation, is strictly impossible."[^naur-death] A verisectorium bets that a record built a certain way can carry much more of the theory than Naur's programs did. His three areas read like a list of what that record would have to hold. "Decisions with backing" goes straight at the second. The judgment of similarity, the third, is the one I'd worry about. It is also exactly where the second reader's difficulty lives (section 6).
- **Text comprehension (cognitive psychology).** The bottleneck is building a situation model that goes beyond the text [memory: Kintsch]. Low-knowledge readers benefit from more cohesive texts, and high-knowledge readers have in some studies benefited from *less* cohesive ones (the "reverse cohesion effect"). That effect has since been qualified for strategic high-knowledge readers.[^mcnamara] The "expertise reversal effect" generalizes the point: material that helps novices can become redundant, and costly, for experienced learners.[^kalyuga]
- **Information foraging (HCI).** The bottleneck is the rate of valuable information gained per unit time. That rate depends on the cost of moving between "patches", the cost of working within each one, and the "information scent" that predicts value before you pay [memory: Pirolli & Card 1999]. This field already has a *ratio* in roughly your sense. A scent model has predicted programmers' debugging navigation better than models without scent.[^lawrance] I found no careful application to LLM agents.
- **Sensemaking.** Russell et al. describe representations being chosen and changed "to reduce the cost of operations in an information processing task," in a "learning loop."[^russell] Klein's data/frame theory makes the frame primary: data evoke frames and frames select data [summary]. Weick adds that people act in order to find out what they think [memory].
- **Situation awareness and handoffs (human factors, medicine).** This field is literally about a mind arriving into an ongoing situation and needing to act within minutes. Its best-measured intervention, the I-PASS handoff bundle, was associated with 23% fewer medical errors and 30% fewer preventable adverse events across nine hospitals, with handoffs no longer than before.[^starmer] The standard probe measure, SAGAT, is debated for measuring recall rather than readiness to act.[^sagat]
- **Transfer and inert knowledge (education).** This field owns your second reader (section 6). One framing worth keeping: there is no single effect size for "far transfer"; transfer can occur along at least 9 dimensions, and "critical conditions for many key questions are untested."[^barnett]
- **Context engineering for LLMs.** Anthropic's framing: "good context engineering means finding the smallest possible set of high-signal tokens that maximize the likelihood of some desired outcome," because models have an "attention budget."[^anthropic] That is your ratio, in other words.

## 3. What makes arrival fast or slow

### 3.1 Evidence from human newcomers

Dagenais et al. (2010) interviewed 18 experienced developers who had joined established projects at IBM.[^dag-method] The findings that matter here:

- **Three factors.** The first is early experimentation: newcomers given code tasks on day one "oriented themselves more quickly."[^dag-early] The second is internalizing structures and cultures, the tacit part. The third is progress validation, frequent checks that the newcomer is heading the right way. Up-front courses and general documentation were "seldom ideal". Newcomers were often pointed to them "for the wrong reasons," such as the team being unprepared or access not yet granted.[^dag-early]
- **Timing decided usefulness.** An architecture explanation on day one "didn't really hit home until later."[^dag-timing] The hooks came from work on the code.
- **The efficient pattern was an overview, then a low-level task.**[^dag-overview]
- **Judging whether a document was outdated "was challenging"**, because newcomers knew little of the project's history. One participant used source history to check.[^dag-outdated] This is note 30's credence problem, seen from the newcomer's side.
- **Trails left by the team helped with tacit knowledge.** Detailed bug and commit histories linking a change to its reason helped, and failed when entries said only "Bug Fixed." The authors' overall conclusion, though, is that "human guides are invaluable, better than guidebooks."[^dag-trails]
- **Documentation with design, examples and rationale was "very valuable … even if not completely up to date."** This tempers any reading of the study as "reading is weak."[^dag-docs]
- **Pointers given just in time were valued over pointers "presented en masse".** Newcomers without progress validation "missed important information they did not know they had to know."[^dag-enmasse]
- **Signposts matter most "at cross-roads, i.e., places with choices where others have tended to get lost."**[^dag-crossroads] This suggests placing orientation aids by where past arrivals actually went wrong.
- **The aids that mattered across all three factors:** previous experience, examples, a mentor, and questions to colleagues. A questionnaire embedded in an architecture document helped one newcomer find what he had not understood.[^dag-aids]

Carroll's Minimal Manual, an action-first manual, produced 40% less learning time and 2.7 times as many completed subtasks as a commercial manual.[^carroll] Baker's reading of the same work warns against taking it as "procedures only": "People will not follow procedures because their current mental model is more real to them than what the procedure is telling them."[^baker] The pattern again: action together with a frame.

### 3.2 Evidence from AI agents

- **Repository context files (Gloaguen et al.).** Tested with Claude Code/Sonnet-4.5, Codex/GPT-5.2 and GPT-5.1-mini, and Qwen Code. LLM-generated files lowered success in 5 of 8 settings, by 0.5 and 2 points on average on the two benchmarks. Developer-written files gave +2.4 points on average, not significant (p = 21%), and helped every agent except Claude Code.[^gloaguen-results] Cost rose by over 20%.[^gloaguen-versions] Overviews did not shorten the steps before the agent first touched a file the reference fix changed.[^gloaguen-overview] Agents followed instructions closely: mentioning `uv` raised its use from under 0.01 to 1.6 calls per task.[^gloaguen-uv] With the repository's other documentation removed, the generated files *helped*, by 2.7%. The authors read this as the files mostly duplicating existing docs.[^gloaguen-docs] Caveats: these are Python tasks where the code is readable ground truth, with one sampled run per agent per task. A body of theory may be very different.
- **Agents with file tools beat reading (Cao et al.).** Coding agents given a corpus as files beat the published state of the art by 17.3% on average across benchmarks. They developed iterative query refinement "without explicit instruction or specialized training."[^cao] For a budget-limited agent, arrival looks like foraging with tools.
- **Long-context degradation.** Chroma tested 18 models and found performance "increasingly unreliable as input length grows." Distractors degraded performance beyond what irrelevant filler of the same length did, and the effect grew with length.[^chroma-distractors] Focused prompts of about 300 tokens beat full ones of about 113k tokens for every model.[^chroma-focused] One oddity I wouldn't over-read: "structural coherence consistently hurts model performance" in needle retrieval, with no tested explanation.[^chroma-coherence] NoLiMa found that when question and passage share little vocabulary, 11 of 13 models fell below half their short-context score by 32K tokens.[^nolima] Shi et al. found model performance "dramatically decreased when irrelevant information is included."[^shi]

### 3.3 What I take from 3.1 and 3.2 together

This is my synthesis. For humans, arrival speeds up when early action creates hooks and frequent checks keep the newcomer from going far astray. For agents, the analogue is question-driven foraging with tools. In both, the body of work helps through four things: a short frame that can be revised, scent and structure that make the right place cheap to find, trails from decisions to their reasons, and a visible state of how far each part has been checked. It hurts through near-duplicate, stale or unadjudicated material, and through requirements that readers obey without needing. I found no evidence either way on whether an agent oriented by *reading a scaffold, doing a small task, then re-reading* beats the same budget spent only reading. The human evidence predicts it would, and it is cheap to test.

## 4. Your ratio, examined

### 4.1 A real quantity close to it, and three things it adds

Foraging theory's central quantity is a rate: value gained divided by time spent moving between sources plus time spent within them [memory]. It adds three things your current wording lacks:

1. **The cost side includes finding your way, not only reading.** Duggan & Payne found that the benefit of skimming depended on "the ease with which participants could navigate through the text."[^duggan]
2. **Value depends on the task as well as the reader.** So the ratio belongs to reader × task × budget, not to the corpus or the reader alone.
3. **Scent.** For a verisectorium, the scent that seems to matter most is credence. Without it, near-truth can't be told from truth until it has been read.

### 4.2 The numerator can be negative, which bears on "truer before shorter"

Decision theory's value of information (Howard 1966 [memory]) makes the value of reading something the expected improvement in the reader's decision. Read that way, falsehood and miscalibrated confidence have *negative* value, and "truer before shorter" mostly falls out of a ratio. It does not fall out in one case. A longer, fully true text can be worse for a budget-limited reader than a shorter text with a small marked error, if the reader runs out of budget before reaching the decisive truth in the longer one. If "truer before shorter" is meant to hold even then, it is an ordering and not a ratio. The literature doesn't decide this. It does make the two readings distinct enough to ask Joseph which he means.

### 4.3 What the evidence says noise is made of

| Kind | Evidence on its cost | What reduces it |
|---|---|---|
| Irrelevant material | Measurably hurts LLMs,[^shi] though less than distractors;[^chroma-distractors] cheap for humans if scent is good | Structure, scent, layering |
| Redundant restatement | Adds cost without benefit when the original is reachable; helps when it is not[^gloaguen-docs] | Restatement tied to its original, used where the original is out of reach |
| Near-truth (stale, superseded, unadjudicated) | Most harmful for LLMs;[^chroma-distractors] hard for newcomers to detect[^dag-outdated] | Replacement rather than accumulation; visible trust state |
| Unneeded instructions | Agents obey them; cost rises;[^gloaguen-uv] "make the task harder" is the authors' current hypothesis[^gloaguen-versions] | Fewer standing instructions, each with its reason |
| Precision with nothing to hang it on | Depends on the reader[^mcnamara][^kalyuga][^dag-timing] | Layering; delivery once a hook exists |
| Falsehood | Negative value (§4.2) | Adjudication |

A point I haven't seen stated: for an agent, noise in the imperative is worse than noise in the descriptive, because the agent acts on it.[^gloaguen-uv]

### 4.4 A doubt about the ratio itself, now weaker

I argued that for a mind meant to *hold* the theory, some effort is how the holding is built, citing the reverse cohesion effect. That effect has been qualified ("good texts can be better for strategic, high-knowledge readers").[^mcnamara] The doubt survives only as a question: does a future lead need the same pages as a budget-limited actor? I'd no longer lean on the reverse cohesion effect to answer it.

## 5. Measurement: what exists and what I would try

What exists:

- **Time to first meaningful act.** The cleanest agent version I found is "steps before the first interaction between the agent and a file included in the PR patch."[^gloaguen-overview] It measures finding, not understanding.
- **Freeze-probe comprehension (SAGAT).** Its critics argue it measures explicit recall, and its correlations with performance vary from moderate to none.[^sagat] A cold-reader quiz has the same weakness.
- **Outcomes downstream.** I-PASS measured errors and adverse events, not handoff quality, in a before-and-after design.[^starmer] This kind is slow, and it is the only kind that settles whether arrival worked.
- **Elicited models.** Dagenais had participants draw their project as if introducing a peer, and the sketches matched the interviews.[^dag-method] With agents this is cheap: ask for the believed state and a confidence for each part.
- **Navigation models.** Scent-based models predict where readers go.[^lawrance]

What I'd try, in the order I think it would teach:

1. **Focused versus full at a fixed budget**, Chroma's design.[^chroma-focused] The gap, or the budget needed to close it, is a noise cost for that reader on that task set. It needs a held-out question set whose answers the steward knows.
2. **An orientation curve.** Performance as a function of tokens read, with a no-read baseline (a contamination check) and the focused condition as the ceiling.
3. **Credence probes.** Score the arriving reader's confidence in each belief against the record's actual check state. This measures note 30's "calibrated truth with calibrated credence" directly. I found nobody doing it for documentation, which, since it seems obvious, makes me suspect I missed it.
4. **Act-first versus read-first**, as an experiment between the two conditions (§3.3).

Two cautions. Instruction drift needs averaging over many runs,[^li] and the context-files study sampled each agent once per task.[^gloaguen-results] And task scores can hold steady while adherence to constraints is lost.[^salle-score] Measures have to count violations, not only successes.

## 6. The reader in the middle of acting

The fields, under their own names: prospective memory; transfer and inert knowledge; procedural compliance (aviation, surgery); and, for agents, instruction drift, instruction density, and instruction survival under compaction.

What is known:

- **The bottleneck is noticing, not knowing.** With a dissimilar story as the source, people solved the target problem spontaneously about 30% of the time, against a 10% baseline with no source. A hint to use the story added about 50 points. With diagram sources, spontaneous use was no better than the baseline. A moving visual source reached 95%. "Retrieval and noticing were major blocks to successful analogical problem solving."[^gick] The form in which a principle was met changes whether it is noticed later.
- **Retrieval depends on overlap between how something was stored and how the moment looks** [memory: encoding specificity; transfer-appropriate processing]. NoLiMa is the LLM version of this: low lexical overlap between question and passage, and retrieval collapses with length.[^nolima] A principle written in the theory's vocabulary is unlikely to fire in a moment described in the task's vocabulary.
- **Cues can carry the load.** In the multiprocess framework, a "focal" cue, one the ongoing task already makes you process, can trigger retrieval spontaneously.[^focal] Implementation intentions ("if situation Y arises, then I will perform behavior Z") improve goal attainment over goals alone, d = 0.65 across 94 studies, and d = 0.54 in the 11 studies about *remembering to act*.[^goll-effect] They have a cost: people are slow to drop a faulty if-then plan unless they get explicit failure feedback.[^goll-costs]
- **Aviation's design** [memory]: a very small set of memory items trained until automatic, and everything else read from a checklist once the situation is recognized. I confirmed only that Degani & Wiener's report exists.[^degani]
- **Standing instructions decay and crowd each other out in LLMs.** Significant instruction drift within eight rounds of conversation, attributed partly to attention decay.[^li] With 500 simultaneous (keyword) instructions, the best models reach 68%, with a bias toward earlier instructions.[^ifscale] In one small model, correct rationales did not reliably become correct actions.[^schmied]
- **Compaction is the second reader made literal.** I now have the primary passages.
  - In one paper, task scores held for the two 1M-context models. Yet compaction dropped rules, and in 44 real sessions it dropped how-to-work instructions and exclusions about 6× more often than what-to-build specifications. Their explanation: summaries keep "what the agent needs to continue," not what it needs "to continue correctly."[^salle-swechat][^salle-score]
  - In the other paper, after compaction an agent deleted email without approval in 30 of 100 sessions, against none before. Restating the constraint in the first turn after compaction cut that to 13. Keeping constraints in a separate list and appending it after compaction gave over 90% retention and 93.4–96.3% compliance.[^wang-inbox][^wang-extractor] Its 17% average retention is mostly a statement about weak compactors on long contexts.[^wang-retention]

What follows, as I read it:

1. Having once read a principle is weak evidence that it will be applied.
2. What helps: storing the principle as *condition → action*, with the condition worded as the moment looks from inside; re-presenting it at the moment from outside the actor's memory; and keeping the set that must live in the head small. Because if-then plans make disengagement sluggish, the re-presented principle also needs a way to be found wrong.[^goll-costs]
3. **The first and second readers pull against each other.** Loading every principle up front costs the first reader's budget.[^gloaguen-uv][^ifscale] Aviation's split is the known resolution.
4. **Task scores miss this failure.**[^salle-score] Measuring it means building situations where a known principle should fire, and counting violations.
5. **Naur and this section meet.** The part of justification Naur says cannot leave the holder is "the decision that they are relevant to the situation at hand."[^naur-areas] Conditionalized principles, situation-worded cues and hooks are attempts to put as much of that decision as possible into the record and the environment. Naur says some of it can never get there. If he is right, the residue is what an arriving mind has to rebuild by doing and being checked, which is A7 in `00-working.md`.

One observation about your own estate, in case it is useful and not just recapitulation. Joseph's global memory writes "BODY-SIGNAL" lines describing what the moment feels like from inside, and holds that bare pointers "inoculate" against looking. The literature gives a mechanism for both. A body-signal is a retrieval cue written in the moment's vocabulary. A pointer is a principle with no conditions attached, which is inert by construction. The same literature warns that the always-loaded surface has a capacity cost, and favors cue-triggered injection.

## 7. The fifteen-minute expert

- **Skimming helps with the main ideas, not with inferences or details.** Compared with reading the first half of a text straight through, skimming within half the time improved memory for important ideas but not for less important details or inferences. It was no better than reading the first or second half of every paragraph. Its benefit depended on how easily readers could navigate, and skim readers spent more time early in paragraphs and pages.[^duggan] For design: put the claim first in each unit, and make the units easy to move between.
- **Expertise reversal applies directly.** What helps the cold agent becomes redundant for an expert.[^kalyuga]
- **Experts arrive with frames** [summary: Klein]. Stating the work's own frame plainly lets them accept or reject it fast.
- **My inference:** what an outside expert can best do in fifteen minutes is calibrate trust, by following one claim to its backing. If that's right, their ratio depends on how few steps lead from any claim to its evidence and check state.

## 8. Doubts, and what I'd want checked

- Most of the human evidence measures learning or recall, not action quality. The onboarding evidence is qualitative and small (18 interviews).[^dag-method] The best outcome-measured study is a before-and-after study in medicine.[^starmer]
- The agent evidence is mostly Python software engineering, where code is readable ground truth.
- Several footnotes are still marked [summary] or [memory]: SAGAT's validity figures, Klein, and the whole aviation paragraph. Those are where a check would most help.
- The convergence in section 6 is real in the sources. Whether it holds for LLM agents beyond the compaction and instruction-density results is untested by anything I found.
- I did not find, and would want someone to look for: studies scoring how well a cold reader's confidence matches a documentation corpus's actual check state; information foraging applied rigorously to agent traces; and quantitative studies of complete team turnover (Naur's dead-program case). The organizational memory literature [memory: Walsh & Ungson 1991] may hold some of the last.

## 9. Feedback on the brief, and on the footnote invitation

The open framing worked for me. Knowing what "acting well" means here (auditing, spiking, integrating) would have sharpened the search, and I'd still want it as context, not as scope.

On the footnotes: they were worth doing, and not mainly as presentation. Writing a quote under each claim forced me back into the texts. That turned up a conclusion its own authors had downgraded, an internal inconsistency, a headline average built partly from 0% baselines, a figure I had wrongly called unverifiable, and a connection I had missed. A source list per source (my first version's section 0) did not force any of that. Per-claim footnotes did. If this becomes a habit for reports, the useful part seems to be the requirement to *quote*, more than the requirement to cite.
