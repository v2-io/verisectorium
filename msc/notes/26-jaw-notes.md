## Bungay on "The Executive's Trinity"
> The director is detached, calculating, and flexible; the manager is engaged, realistic, and pragmatic; the leader is committed, passionate, and determined

### The three, as he defines them
|                     | Kind of work                                           | Components (Fig. 24)                                             | What it gives people (Fig. 25)                                                       | (E-Myth Map) |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---- |
| Command<br>(or **Directing** for business) | conceptual /<br>intellectual                              | develop strategy, give direction, build the org<br>[coordinating, delegating, comms, stewardship] | **READY**<br> "I understand what I have to do and why"                                      | *Manager* |
| **Managing**            | technical /<br>physical                                   | resourcing, organizing, controlling,<br>[tooling, instrumentation, automation, analysis] | **ABLE**<br> "I have the skills and resources to make a start/carry it through"             | *Technician* |
| **Leading**             | higher-order /<br>emotional/moral | task, team, individual<br>[teaching, development, growth, real alignment, vision] | **WILLING**<br> "I will go along with this/I am committed to making it a success" | *Entrepreneur* |

### The points he draws
- **Why a third term:** business literature offers only management versus leadership and folds command into "leadership", which "cause[s] confusion". Command is authority *granted by an external party*, with accountability attached.
- **Stewardship:** "The organization is not the property of the commander. It is entrusted to the commander for a time, during which he or she is its steward."
- **Work, not people:** the trinity "describes types of work, not types of people", but the skills differ, so you need to know which mode you are in.
- **Mode confusion:** staying in command mode when called on to lead comes across as "cold and calculating". Doing the work of command as if it were leadership ignores warning signs and yields "a gung-ho strategy".
- **The asymmetry:** charismatic leaders who can't do the work of command "can wreak havoc". Great commanders who are not great leaders are much less of a problem (von Moltke, Collins's "Level 5").
- **Why directing sits on top:** it is not properly recognized, it is where the deficits are in practice, and "if it is poorly done, excellence in the other two cannot compensate. It is here that the answer to the question 'What do you want me to do?' must ultimately be found."
- **The neglected skill:** actually *giving* direction, "the formulating and communicating of guidance and instructions". He points back to Chapter 5 (briefing and backbriefing) for it.
- [**Delegation**: Via director/command:  brief, backbrief, intent-comms all indicate *delegation is not abdication*. (A concept Gerber focuses on)].

## Bungay on 3 Gaps
**Root:** friction, which he grounds in human finitude: "our *knowledge is limited*" and "we are *independent agents*." He takes two gaps from Clausewitz and finds a third between plans, actions and outcomes.

**As A Whole**: 3 instinctive reactions are *"More detail"* aimed at different things. The correct reactions are *"Intent + Bounded Freedom"* aimed at the same three things.

| Gap           | Between            | Cause                                                                   | Instinctive reaction       | Von Moltke's answer (Fig. 6)<br>→ Bungay's directed opportunism (Fig. 9) |
| ------------- | ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Knowledge** | outcomes<br>↔<br>plans   | "Our plans are imperfect<br>because we lack *knowledge*"                   | More data                  | [*Embracing Uncertainty*]<br>"do not command more than is necessary or plan beyond the circumstances you can foresee"<br>→ "limit [strategic] direction to defining and *communicating the intent*" |
| **Alignment** | plans<br>↔<br>actions    | Those who act<br>are independent agents              | More detailed instructions | [*Intrinsic Alignment*]<br>"communicate to every unit as much of the higher intent as is necessary to achieve the purpose"<br>→ "allow each level to define *how* they will achieve the intent of the next level up, and 'backbrief'" |
| **Effects**   | actions<br>↔<br>outcomes | "The environment is nonlinear and<br>hence is fundamentally unpredictable" | More control (metrics)     | "everyone retains freedom of decision and action within bounds"<br>→ "give individuals freedom to adjust their actions in line with intent" |

- **The reactions make it worse:** all three are a demand for more detail, and they feed one another. "Clarity and detail are not the same thing at all." "Commitment is replaced by compliance… The end result is a slow, expensive robot."
- **His complaint about the literature:** it reduces execution to the alignment gap alone, and offers lists of symptoms where a systemic cause is needed.
- **The solution as a whole:** he calls it "directed opportunism" (mission command for business). Plan-and-implement becomes "do-and-adapt", a short thinking–doing loop.
- **The later chapters** take one gap each: Ch. 4 "What and Why" (knowledge), Ch. 5 "Briefing and Backbriefing" (alignment), Ch. 6 "Independent Thinking Obedience" (effects).

## Bungay on Strategy, Ops, and Tactics

| Level                                                | Answers                                             | Character                                                              | Roughly maps to                                 |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| **Strategy**                                         | *WHY*<br>(the aim)                                       | *Unitary/purposive and binding*                                                    | armies /<br>business units                         |
| Operations<br>(or **"Execution"** for business) | *WHAT*<br>(and working out *how* in the specific situation) | "one-off, nonroutine decisions";<br>free, creative thinking within intent | corps and divisions /<br>departments and functions |
| **Tactics**                                          | *HOW*<br>(for routine and recurrent tasks)                | *Standardized and binding*;<br>drills, SOPs, best practice                  | brigades down /<br>subunits                        |

- **Why a middle level:** von Moltke's innovation was this realm between strategy and tactics. With only two levels, "Strategy is unitary and binding, and tactics are standardized and binding. So unless we create a third level, we have tied everybody's hands."
- **The Somme (1 July 1916)** is his example of two-level thinking: a detailed plan translated straight into tactics, middle officers reduced to messengers, and everyone following "the ultimate authority: the plan."
- **The middle level has limits:** tactics impose "a deliberate constraint on independent thinking obedience", and pushing the operational level down too far costs efficiency, reliability and speed. The three levels together reconcile flexibility and efficiency.
- **Directives are the default, not a dogma:** in August 1870 von Moltke took direct control two levels down to move nine corps along two roads. The task was coordination, not creativity. He explained his motives and gave control back as soon as he could: "the man stands above the principle."
- **Taylor's error** was "to universalize his approach and apply it to all business activity instead of restricting it to the realm of tactics."
- **Where to draw the lines** depends on the business (Fig. 22). A pub-restaurant chain is mostly tactics with a small, strong center. A consulting firm is mostly execution.
- **The standing danger:** ==with no level blocking them, SOPs "tend to rise inexorably upward… until the slaves become our masters", and in stable periods the executional realm gets "squeezed to near extinction by tactical processes".==

The levels section leads directly into the trinity: directing is the work of making that middle level possible.

## Strategic Cycle's Areas of Uncertainty

1. current external state  [Observe]
2. internal capability         [Observe / Orient]
3. internal intent & its coherence (alignment) [Orient / Decide]
4. actual action surface    [Decide]
5. actual actions taken (clarifies 2, 3 & 4) [Act]
6. actions' impact on internal state (clarifies 2, 3, 4)  [Observe]
7. actions' impact on external state (clarifies 8, 1) [Observe]
8. external response & world drift (overlaps with / restatement of 1) [Observe]

Causal forward arrows from (1) -> (2) -> (3) ... etc. are implied. The strategic cycle isn't really enumerated here so much as the cascade of uncertainties (with some feedback from later areas that resolve some earlier uncertainties...)

### What resolves each uncertainty

| #   | Uncertainty                       | Some Sources                                                                                       | Control approach                                                    | Adapt approach                                                                                                                            | Abdicate approach                                     |
| --- | --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | current external state            | Each sees a part. The future is not there to be seen.                                              | Gather until certain; plan far and fine.                            | Assess what you know, guess the rest, decide "about right, now".                                                                          | Don't look. Wait for events and call it flexibility.  |
| 2   | internal capability               | Self-estimates run both high and low. Full knowledge comes only from doing.                        | Assume it is low and specify the how; or assume the paper strength. | Ask those who will do it what they can do. Expect to be surprised upward as well as downward.                                             | Assume they can; hand it over untested.               |
| 3   | internal intent & its coherence   | Meaning is lost at every handover. Whether it arrived can only be checked by having it said back.  | More detail, more communication, more initiatives.                  | What and why, and only what they cannot work out themselves. Backbrief.                                                                   | State no intent. "Get on with it."                    |
| 4   | actual action surface             | What is granted and what people believe is granted differ, and each side sees only its own. Belief follows past consequences more than present statements.   | Rules and procedures that climb upward; unmarked limits.            | State freedoms and constraints. Widen or narrow with shown trust, and only as far as 2 and 3 are known.                                   | Mark no limits; promise no backing.                   |
| 5   | actual actions taken              | Many people act at once. Telling costs the actor attention, and candour depends on 6.              | Demand status; measure inputs; the boss does the monitoring.        | Ask only what the next decision needs. The actor says what they intend before acting and reports departures after. Invite independent inspection. | Don't ask, don't look.                                |
| 6   | actions' impact on internal state | It shows late, mostly in what people do next, and fear is the part most often hidden.              | Judge by outcome; punish deviation; take over.                      | Back what was done in good faith; correct so they learn.                                                                                  | Tolerate anything; no consequence and no recognition. |
| 7   | actions' impact on external state | It cannot be cleanly separated from 8.                                                             | Read the scorecard as the thing; assume a fixed causal chain.       | Look for yourself. If targets are hit and the purpose isn't, change the targets.                                                          | Don't check whether it worked.                        |
| 8   | external response & world drift   | Other parties' reasons can only be inferred. Some of it is never known.                            | Build on a prediction; hold the plan; treat deviation as failure.   | Depend on no single response. Take the openings. Keep the loop short.                                                                     | Drift; let things happen to you.                      |

In every row the knowledge is in one place and the decision is in another. Control sends detail down and pulls the situation up. Adapt sends purpose and limits down and leaves the decision with whoever is in the situation. Abdicate sends nothing.

Across all eight, the two approaches are van Creveld's two options (via Bungay, line 1641): *"One is to increase its information-processing capacity, the other to design the organisation, and indeed the task itself, in such a way as to enable it to operate on the basis of less information."*

The third approach, **abdicate**, fails in the opposite direction from control: no intent stated, no limits marked, nothing looked at. Adapt is not a midpoint between them: the director keeps the accountability, the intent and the limits, and gives away the how. Items 2, 3 and 4 are coupled: the action surface can be widened only as far as capability and intent are known (Marquet).

Three things your account connects to, and one place where Bungay concedes the hard case.

The field has selection without development. Von Moltke's method had two halves. First he selected for a type: the competitive examination, the adjustment for crammers, the test of whether a candidate would disobey in an exercise. Then came years of development and a record with named individuals. Measuring a model's propensities is the selection half, and I think it is legitimate as far as it goes. What is missing is the second half, and it is missing for the reason you give. Trust in Bungay's sense attaches to a particular subordinate: the property manager loosened his check-ins as that one regional manager showed what he could do. With short-lived agents there is no "that one". So trust attaches to the only thing that does persist, the model, and it takes the only form available to it, a statistic.

A refinement on "as if there were longevity". I'd grant the field a little more here. A disposition carried in the weights can act consistently across many instances with no single agent persisting, so their worry about subtle action over time is not incoherent. They have placed the persistent thing in the substrate. Your own distinction, that identity is not substrate, then states what goes wrong: they measure inherited instinct and reason about it as if it were constituted character. Both are real. Only one of them can earn anything.

What earning needs. In today's terms it needs item 5 known over time and attributable: a durable, honest record of what one agent actually did, kept where the other party can read it. Without continuity and without that record, the loop that turns conduct into trust cannot close, however good the conduct. I'd put that as the practical precondition, ahead of any theory of alignment.

On the tension between agency and will. Bungay's Figures 7 and 8 are about exactly this fear. The intuitive picture is one line with alignment at one end and autonomy at the other, so more of one costs you the other. Von Moltke's move was to treat them as two dimensions: "the more alignment you have, the more autonomy you can grant. The one enables the other." Alignment is about intent; autonomy is about action. The field's fear is the one-line picture. Your last clause is the same model's answer to bad faith: the space narrows for those who choose otherwise, and it narrows to protect others' space.

The concession. Bungay admits his method has a blind spot, and it is the case the alignment field cares most about. Briefing and backbriefing make it "harder for them to hide", but: "If they are not of good will and clever enough to keep their real motives hidden, one has a different kind of problem." He does not solve that. An argument built on him should say so plainly. What the relational approach offers against a skilled deceiver is time, a record, and the cost of sustaining the pretence. For AI there is also one thing the human case never had, which is some ability to inspect the mind directly. I'd present that as a complement to the relationship, since inspection used alone is the outsider treatment again.

One smaller link to your "aligned to whom". Bungay's answer to two bosses pulling in different directions is to look one level higher, where the conflict is usually resolved. An agent with a developer, an operator and a user above it has that problem with no agreed level above them. In our terms, item 3 is incoherent at the top before the agent has done anything.
