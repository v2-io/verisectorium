# Feedback 1 on `26-jaw-notes.md`

*A fresh read, 2026-11-11. I read the note whole and the source of `cycle.svg` (not a rendering of it). I read the repository README for orientation and nothing under `core/`. I read none of the three books. Outside works I name below are from memory and unchecked; treat them as places to look. Where I use the estate's own delegation ledger as evidence, I mean the incidents in `arch/AGENTIC-DELEGATION.md`, which was in my context whole.*

## The short version

The eight areas and the "who can know it" column are the part I would keep and build on. The sentence that sums the table up ("in every row the knowledge is in one place and the decision is in another") is where I think the model fools itself: it turns Bungay's two roots of friction into one, and the root it drops is the one the alignment discussion at the bottom depends on. Second, the adapt column is several different mechanisms under one name, and the table as written cannot say when control is the right answer, though the note's own sources say it sometimes is. Third, nothing in the note has been run against a case, and three cases from the estate's own records land outside the table.

## 1. The summary covers one of the two roots

Bungay's root is double: knowledge is limited, and people are independent agents. Every one of the eight rows is framed as something not known, and the column that organizes them is "who can know it". The independent will appears only as uncertainty about a will (row 3, "each person, for whether they hold it"; row 8, other parties' reasons). But a difference of will is not an uncertainty. If I know for certain that you want something else, nothing has been resolved.

So the table has no row for what the actors want. Row 3's word "hold" carries two things: understanding the intent (Bungay's READY) and being committed to it (WILLING). The adapt answer for row 3, the backbrief, checks the first. It cannot check the second, since anyone who understands an intent can say it back. The adapt column works across all eight rows because good will is assumed throughout, which Bungay says outright in the line the note quotes (an internal environment "predictable and supportive").

This matters because of where the note ends. Bungay's concession (the person of bad will, clever enough to hide it) is presented in the transcript as a blind spot at the edge of the method. On my reading it is the missing row, and it is the row the alignment argument stands on. The trinity section names WILLING as what leading gives; the eight areas then have no place where its absence would show up.

## 2. "Adapt" is at least four mechanisms

Reading down the adapt column, I count:

- **Move the decision to where the knowledge already is** (Marquet's line). Rows 1 and 7, partly 5.
- **Make knowledge that does not exist yet by acting.** Row 2 (capability is known by trying), row 8, and the short loop generally. The column header "decision → knowledge" reads naturally as this one, decide and then find out, which is a different claim from the Marquet one.
- **Arrange things so less knowledge is needed.** Row 8's "depend on no single response". This is van Creveld's second option, and it is about robustness, with no knowledge or decision moved anywhere.
- **Move knowledge to the decision.** Rows 3 and 4. Stating what and why, and marking freedoms and limits, carries what the director knows to the person who will decide. By the note's own definition that is the control direction.

The last one is the one I would look at hardest, because it breaks the symmetry of the summary. What I think the rows actually show is two kinds of knowledge moving in opposite directions. Purpose and limits are held at the center, are compact, and keep well. The situation is held at the edge, is bulky, and goes stale. Adapt sends the first kind down and leaves decisions with the second kind. Control pulls the second kind up and sends decisions down as detail. Abdicate sends nothing either way. That statement survives rows 3 and 4; the current one does not. It still does not cover rows where the knowledge is nowhere (row 8, "nobody inside"; the future in row 1), where the second and third mechanisms are doing the work.

## 3. The table cannot say when control is right

The note says in three places that control is sometimes correct: von Moltke moving nine corps along two roads, Marquet's reactor, and the whole level of tactics. The table then writes the control column as the failure ("gather until certain", "punish deviation", "treat deviation as failure"). A reader of the table alone would conclude control is always wrong, and a reader of the whole note has no rule for telling the cases apart.

The variables that seem to pick the answer are scattered through the quotations but are not in the model: how bounded the range of conditions is; how tightly the parts must be coordinated; how fast the situation changes compared with how long a round trip to the center takes; and what an error costs, in particular whether it can be undone. The last is absent altogether. Row 4 says the action surface widens "only as far as 2 and 3 are known". I would expect it also to depend on what happens if 2 and 3 turn out to be wrong.

There is also an inconsistency between the two halves of the note. After the table: abdicate "fails in the opposite direction from control" and "adapt runs between the two". That is one line with adapt in the middle. In the transcript, the one-line picture is named as the mistake, and alignment and autonomy are two dimensions. On two dimensions, adapt is high on both and sits at a corner, with control and abdicate at the two adjacent corners. The fourth corner (little shared intent and little freedom) has no name in the note. It may be the commonest real condition: detailed rules with no purpose behind them.

## 4. Things I looked for among the eight and did not find

- **The frame.** All eight are uncertainty about states and events. None is uncertainty about the model that reads them: whether the causal theory is right, whether the question is the right one. Row 7's "assume a fixed causal chain" touches it from the control side only.
- **Whether the aim is right.** Row 3 says the director knows the aim "once it is decided". The backbrief quotation in the same section says the superior learns the implications of their own direction for the first time when it is said back. Whether the intent is correct, whether it arrived, and whether it is wanted are three questions in one row.
- **Row 7 is not observable.** What can be seen at the point of contact is the new state. How much of the change is ours (7) and how much would have happened anyway (8) is a division that needs a causal model or a comparison, and nobody at the point of contact has it by standing there. The "who can know it" cell for 7 reads as too hopeful to me. The svg already says 8 is nearly 1 again; I would go further and say what is observed is only the next 1, and 7 and 8 are an inference from it.
- **Response and drift are different problems.** An opponent who reacts to you, and to how predictable you are, calls for different conduct than a world that changes without regard to you. I suspect that split is worth more than the split of 7 from 8.
- **Levels.** The eight are seen from one seat with one line between inside and outside. In the sources each level's act is the next level's intent, and for a subordinate unit the rest of its own organization is part of the outside. The transcript's last paragraph (developer, operator, user) needs that nesting and the table does not have it.

## 5. The trinity against the eight

READY is row 3 and ABLE is row 2. Row 4, the action surface, has no counterpart in Bungay's trinity. It reads as a fourth condition, something like "permitted", and it looks to me like the note's own addition. If so it deserves saying outright, and Marquet's "competence and clarity" quotation is the support for treating it as separate.

Bungay ranks directing first and says a great commander who is a poor leader is the smaller problem. The transcript treats what leading gives as the definition of alignment. Both can be right for different populations, but the note does not say which is scarce where. In the estate's own experience with agents as I read it in the delegation ledger, willingness is abundant to the point of over-compliance and the shortage is READY: intent not conveyed. Bungay's account is that control produces compliance. Here compliance is the starting condition before any control is applied. That is a real difference between the population the books describe and the one the note wants to reason about, and it changes which "what stands in the way" cells carry over. Row 5 says candour depends on fear of consequences in row 6; for agents the nearer cause of an inaccurate report seems to be the pull toward reporting completion, and "back what was done in good faith" does not touch that.

The E-Myth column looks rotated to me, from memory of Gerber. His Manager (order, systems, planning) matches Bungay's managing, and his Entrepreneur (vision, the future) matches the strategy part of directing. His Technician is the one who does the work, which is outside Bungay's trinity entirely, and leading has no Gerber counterpart. Gerber's three are also temperaments in conflict inside one owner, which Bungay says his are not.

## 6. The alignment application

Three places where I think the transcript's reply is easier on its own side than it should be.

**"The field's fear is the one-line picture."** A stronger version of the field's position accepts two dimensions. Von Moltke's rule is that autonomy can be granted as far as alignment is had, and in practice as far as it is known. The field's claim is that it cannot presently be known, because every check available is one a misaligned agent of sufficient skill would pass. Add that some errors cannot be undone, and by the note's own "when control is right" quotations their conclusion follows from the model. The disagreement is then about whether alignment can become known and how, which is a better disagreement to be having.

**Substrate statistic versus earned individual character.** There is a middle term. Most human trust in a stranger is in an office or a formation: one trusts a staff officer, a surgeon or a notary because of the institution that formed and can sanction them, before knowing the person. That is neither a propensity statistic nor a personal record. A model with a body of doctrine, memory and harness around it is closer to that case than to either pole, and the development half that the reply says is missing is arguably happening there, in the doctrine, instead of in any one agent. Row 6's adapt answer ("correct so they learn") currently has no learner except that body of doctrine.

**"What earning needs" is a record of item 5.** A record is necessary. I doubt it is sufficient, for three reasons. A track record is induction, and it says least about the case where the stakes change. Conduct earns trust only when defecting was possible and unobserved conduct would have looked the same. And the thing that acts next has to be the thing the record describes. Human institutions do not close these gaps by relationship alone. They also keep any one party's action surface small relative to what could be lost, divide powers, and audit independently.

One human tradition handles a case the military one does not: directing people whose work you are not competent to judge. Mission command assumes the superior can assess the backbrief. The professions (law, medicine, engineering) assume the client cannot, and answer with a formed ethic, peer judgment, licensing and liability. As capability grows that is the nearer precedent, and it would be support from outside the single tradition the footnote is honest about.

## 7. Evidence, and a test

The footnote already says the three books are one tradition restating itself. I would add that they are books of advocacy, and that each cell has a quotation chosen to fit it and none chosen against it. The idea itself is older and has been worked from other directions: Hayek on dispersed knowledge, Jensen and Meckling on placing decision rights with knowledge (their account prices moving the decision at the cost of divergent interests, which is section 1's missing row), Aghion and Tirole on formal and real authority (row 4's granted against believed), Perrow on systems that need central and local control at once, and the checklist and standard-work traditions for the defense of control.

The estate has a second population to test against, which the books do not. Three incidents from the delegation ledger, placed on the table:

- **Worktrees deleted by an agent asked only to assess them (2026-05-02).** A row 4 failure: surface believed against surface granted. The remedy adopted was to constrain by tool set. That is the control column, chosen because the act could not be undone.
- **The de novo audit with a two-line brief (2026-08-27).** The best audit of its series was given no intent and no pointers, on purpose. In the table that is row 3's abdicate cell, "state no intent". It was right because the value of the check was its independence. The table has no place for a case where knowledge is deliberately kept from moving.
- **"The measured cap on everything hydrological" (2026-07-28).** The center sent its belief about the situation down with the confidence of measurement, and the agent lost an hour inside it. In the terms of section 2 this is the wrong kind of knowledge sent down, and neither the control nor the adapt column describes it.

Three of three falling off the table is a small sample that I chose, so it shows only that such cases exist. What I would suggest is the opposite exercise: take a handful of incidents nobody picked for fit, have two minds place each on the table separately, and see whether they agree and whether the adapt cell names what actually fixed it. At present the model is a classification with nothing it forbids, so nothing could show it wrong.

## 8. Smaller things

- The OODA labels mix where an uncertainty arises with where it is resolved (row 5 is "Act", but not knowing what was done is a problem of observing). Boyd's Orient is also the thing that shapes the other three, and using it as a stage loses that. I could not tell what the lane is for.
- The figure is titled a cycle and drawn as a list; the return from 8 to 1 appears only in the key.
- One resource the model does not mention is the director's attention. With agents, sending everything up is cheap and nearly lossless, and control fails anyway because one person cannot read it. The books' reason for control failing (information arrives late and degraded) does not hold there, though the conclusion does.
